      Painkillers explained: paracetamol, ibuprofen, naproxen and mefenamic acid — which to use, safe doses and who should avoid them

      Paracetamol and anti-inflammatory painkillers (NSAIDs) such as ibuprofen, naproxen and mefenamic acid are safe and effective for most people when used correctly. But they aren’t interchangeable. NSAIDs can cause stomach bleeding and kidney problems, aren’t safe in dengue or late pregnancy, and paracetamol overdose can damage the liver even when you feel fine. This is a general guide for adults. Always read the label and ask a pharmacist.

      Key points
      • Paracetamol: adults usually take 500 mg to 1 g at least 4 hours apart, no more than 4 g (eight 500 mg tablets) in 24 hours. Many cold remedies also contain paracetamol, so count it all.
      • Ibuprofen and other NSAIDs work well for inflammation-type pain, such as period pain, dental pain and sprains. Take them with food, at the lowest dose for the shortest time.
      • Avoid NSAIDs if dengue is possible, if you have a stomach ulcer, kidney or heart disease, are taking blood thinners, or are 20 or more weeks pregnant.
      • Taking paracetamol and ibuprofen together gave good relief for about 7 in 10 people in studies of short-term pain.
      • A paracetamol overdose needs emergency care even if you feel well. Delaying treatment by more than 24 hours greatly raises the risk of liver damage.

      How well they work: a 2015 Cochrane overview of non-prescription painkillers, mostly tested on pain after dental surgery, found ibuprofen with paracetamol worked for about 7 in 10 people, and fast-acting ibuprofen also worked well. Paracetamol alone was less effective for this type of pain. Adults can take paracetamol and ibuprofen together if needed, as they work differently.

      Paracetamol: safe use

      • Usual adult dose: one or two 500 mg tablets at a time, at least 4 hours apart, and no more than 8 tablets (4 g) in 24 hours.
      • You may need a lower dose if you weigh under 50 kg, have liver or kidney problems, or drink a lot of alcohol. Ask a pharmacist.
      • Check for hidden paracetamol. Many cold and flu remedies contain it. Taking them with paracetamol tablets is a common way to overdose by accident.
      • See a doctor if you still need it after 3 days.

      Overdose is dangerous even if you feel fine. Paracetamol overdose can cause liver failure, and symptoms may not appear for a day or more. In a study of more than 1,000 overdose cases at two Kuala Lumpur hospitals, treatment started more than 24 hours after the overdose raised the risk of liver damage about 9-fold. If you have taken too much, go to an emergency department straight away. The National Poison Centre (+604 653 6999) can also advise.

      NSAIDs: ibuprofen, naproxen and mefenamic acid

      DiclofenacAs prescribedNot for people with heart disease, stroke, poor circulation or heart failure
      • Take them with or after food, at the lowest dose for the shortest time.
      • Don’t take two NSAIDs together, for example ibuprofen with naproxen.
      • At high doses (2,400 mg of ibuprofen a day or more), NSAIDs slightly increase the risk of heart attack and stroke. This risk isn’t seen with ibuprofen at up to 1,200 mg a day.
      • NSAID gels rubbed on painful joints, such as in knee arthritis, help some people and are an option for those who can’t take tablets.

      Who should avoid NSAIDs

      Don’t take ibuprofen, naproxen, mefenamic acid or diclofenac without a doctor’s advice if you:

      • might have dengue. In Malaysia, use paracetamol for any fever until dengue is ruled out. See our dengue guide.
      • have or have had a stomach ulcer or bleeding. See our guide to reflux and ulcers.
      • have kidney disease, heart failure, heart disease, or have had a stroke
      • have high blood pressure that isn’t controlled
      • take blood-thinning medicines, steroids or some antidepressants
      • have asthma that gets worse with aspirin or NSAIDs
      • are 20 or more weeks pregnant. The US Food and Drug Administration warns NSAIDs at this stage can harm the baby’s kidneys.
      • are dehydrated, for example with vomiting or diarrhoea
      • are over 65, without checking with a doctor first

      A Malaysian national survey found that people who reported kidney disease were more likely to use NSAIDs, suggesting many don’t know they should avoid them.

      Stronger painkillers

      Painkillers containing codeine or tramadol are prescription medicines. They commonly cause constipation and drowsiness, and can cause dependence with regular use, so they are used for short periods. Codeine isn’t usually used while breastfeeding. Don’t drive if they make you drowsy.

      Be careful with “pain remedies” sold online

      The Ministry of Health has repeatedly warned about unregistered traditional products, sold for joint or body pain, that were found to contain hidden steroids such as dexamethasone, and NSAIDs such as diclofenac. Taken for long periods, hidden steroids can cause high blood sugar and blood pressure, weak bones and other serious problems.

      • Buy medicines and traditional products only if they have a MAL registration number and security hologram. You can check a product on the NPRA website.
      • Ask a pharmacist before buying painkillers online.

      When to see a doctor

      • pain that hasn’t improved after 3 days of paracetamol or 10 days of ibuprofen
      • you need painkillers most days
      • you have black stools, vomit blood, or severe stomach pain while taking an NSAID
      • you have signs of an allergy, such as swelling or difficulty breathing (call 999)
      • any suspected paracetamol overdose, even if you feel well (go to an emergency department)

      Where to get advice about pain

      A GP can find the cause of ongoing pain, choose a painkiller that is safe with your health conditions and other medicines, and prescribe stronger treatment if needed. Our directory lists GP clinics across Kuala Lumpur, Selangor and other states, including clinics open 24 hours.

      Frequently asked questions

      What is the difference between paracetamol and ibuprofen?

      Paracetamol eases pain and fever and is gentle on the stomach. Ibuprofen is an anti-inflammatory that works well for inflammation-type pain but can irritate the stomach and isn’t safe in dengue.

      How much paracetamol can I take in a day?

      For most adults, no more than 4 g (eight 500 mg tablets) in 24 hours, with at least 4 hours between doses. Less if you weigh under 50 kg or have liver problems.

      Can I take paracetamol and ibuprofen together?

      Yes, adults can take them together or alternate them, as they work differently. Stick to the maximum dose of each.

      Can I take ibuprofen for dengue fever?

      No. Ibuprofen and other NSAIDs increase bleeding risk in dengue. Use paracetamol for any fever until dengue is ruled out.

      What is mefenamic acid used for?

      Mainly period pain. The usual adult dose is 500 mg three times a day, taken with food.

      Is ibuprofen safe in pregnancy?

      Avoid it from 20 weeks of pregnancy unless a doctor advises. Paracetamol is usually the preferred painkiller in pregnancy.

      What should I do if I took too much paracetamol?

      Go to an emergency department straight away, even if you feel well. Liver damage may not show for a day or more, and early treatment works best.

      Which painkiller is best for toothache or period pain?

      Anti-inflammatories such as ibuprofen often work best, and combining ibuprofen with paracetamol is very effective, if NSAIDs are safe for you.
