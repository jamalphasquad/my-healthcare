      Ubat sakit tekak: sore throat remedies that work, lozenges and sprays, and when you need a doctor

      Sakit tekak means sore throat. Most are caused by a cold or flu virus and clear up within about a week, with or without antibiotics. The aim is to ease the pain while your body fights the infection. Here is what works, what to buy at the pharmacy, who should avoid certain products, and when a sore throat needs a doctor.

      Key points
      • Most sore throats are viral and last about a week. Most people recover without antibiotics, even when bacteria are the cause.
      • Paracetamol is the safest painkiller to start with. Ibuprofen can help too, but avoid it if you might have dengue.
      • Medicated lozenges and sprays reduce pain a little. Flurbiprofen lozenges are for ages 12 and over, and no lozenges should be given to children under 4.
      • Cold drinks, ice lollies, soft food and, for adults, salt water gargles can ease the pain. Honey may help, but never give it to babies under 1.
      • Get urgent help for difficulty breathing or swallowing, drooling, noisy breathing, or one-sided swelling at the back of the mouth. People taking carbimazole should get urgent advice for any sore throat.

      What causes a sore throat?

      • Viruses: the most common cause, including colds (selsema) and flu
      • Bacteria: mainly group A streptococcus (strep throat). See our tonsillitis guide.
      • Irritation: smoking or vaping, second-hand smoke, shouting or overusing your voice, dry air and mouth breathing
      • Acid reflux, especially with a sore throat in the morning. See our GERD guide.
      • Allergies and post-nasal drip. See our resdung guide.
      • Haze: the Ministry of Health lists sore throat among the effects of haze, and advises staying indoors, drinking plenty of water and wearing a mask outdoors. See our haze guide.

      What helps a sore throat

      Painkillers:

      • Paracetamol for pain or fever. Check the label of any cold remedy, as many already contain paracetamol.
      • Ibuprofen can also help if it suits you. Avoid it if you might have dengue, if you have a stomach ulcer, or in late pregnancy. See our painkiller guide.

      Home measures:

      • Cold or warm drinks, whichever feels better, and plenty of fluids
      • Ice cubes, ice lollies and soft, cool foods. Avoid small hard sweets for young children, as they can cause choking.
      • Salt water gargle for adults: half a teaspoon of salt in a glass of warm water. Don’t ask children to gargle.
      • Honey may help symptoms of colds, and is commonly used in warm water. Don’t give honey to babies under 1.
      • Rest your voice and avoid smoke.

      Traditional remedies such as asam jawa, halia (ginger) and limau are widely used. We found no good trials of them for sore throat. They are generally harmless in food amounts, but don’t rely on them if you have warning signs.

      Lozenges and sprays: are they worth buying?

      UK guidance says medicated lozenges containing a local anaesthetic, an anti-inflammatory or an antiseptic may reduce sore throat pain by a small amount in adults. There is no evidence that ordinary sweets, plain lozenges or mouthwashes help.

      Product typeGood to know
      Anti-inflammatory lozenge (flurbiprofen)Ages 12 and over. Up to 5 a day for no more than 3 days. Not for people allergic to aspirin or anti-inflammatories, with a stomach ulcer, or in late pregnancy. Up to half of users notice taste changes, numbness or nausea.
      Antiseptic lozenge (such as amylmetacresol with dichlorobenzyl alcohol)Not for children under 6. See a doctor if you aren’t better after 3 days.
      Anti-inflammatory spray (benzydamine)Dose depends on age and weight. Use with caution if you have asthma.
      Numbing products (benzocaine, lidocaine)Benzocaine shouldn’t be used in children under 2, because of a rare but dangerous blood reaction.

      Children: don’t give any lozenge to a child under 4, because of the choking risk. Ask a pharmacist which products suit your child’s age.

      Do I need antibiotics?

      Usually not. Antibiotics shorten a sore throat by only about 16 hours on average, and most people get better without them. In a Malaysian survey, 79% of people wrongly believed antibiotics cure viral infections.

      Malaysia’s national antimicrobial guideline uses a scoring system based on fever, swollen neck glands, pus on the tonsils, the absence of a cough, and age. A doctor considers antibiotics, usually penicillin or amoxicillin, only when the score is high enough to suggest strep throat. See our tonsillitis guide for details.

      Steroids: a single dose of a steroid can ease severe sore throat pain a little faster. A 2017 BMJ guideline suggests it as an option for some people aged 5 and over, but UK guidance doesn’t recommend it routinely. It is a decision for your doctor.

      When to get help

      Call 999 or go to an emergency department for:

      • difficulty breathing, or high-pitched noisy breathing
      • being unable to swallow, or drooling
      • symptoms getting worse very quickly

      See a doctor urgently for:

      • swelling on one side at the back of the mouth, or difficulty speaking or opening your mouth. This can be a throat abscess (quinsy).
      • a sore throat, fever or mouth ulcers if you take carbimazole for an overactive thyroid. Stop the medicine and get urgent advice, as it can rarely affect your white blood cells. See our thyroid guide.
      • a baby under 3 months with a temperature of 38°C or more

      See a doctor if:

      • your sore throat hasn’t improved after a week
      • you have a weakened immune system
      • you have a rash or swollen joints
      • your child is under 5 and unwell

      A sore throat can also be part of a flu-like illness a few weeks after HIV infection, and some sexually transmitted infections can affect the throat. If you are worried, see our guides to HIV and STI testing.

      Find a clinic for a sore throat

      A GP can examine your throat, decide whether you need antibiotics, and issue an MC if you need time off work. Our directory lists clinics across Malaysia, including those open late, at weekends and 24 hours.

      Frequently asked questions

      What is sakit tekak in English?

      Sore throat. Ubat sakit tekak means sore throat medicine.

      What is the best ubat sakit tekak?

      Paracetamol is a good first choice for pain. Medicated lozenges or sprays can help a little in adults. Cold drinks, ice lollies and salt water gargles also help. Avoid ibuprofen if you might have dengue.

      How can I get rid of a sore throat fast?

      There is no instant cure, as most sore throats are viral and last about a week. Painkillers, cool drinks, lozenges and resting your voice ease the pain while it heals.

      Do I need antibiotics for a sore throat?

      Usually not. Antibiotics only shorten symptoms by about 16 hours and are used when a doctor suspects strep throat.

      Can I give my child lozenges?

      Not under 4, because of the choking risk. Many medicated lozenges are only for children over 6 or 12. Ask a pharmacist.

      Does salt water help a sore throat?

      Many people find it soothing. Use half a teaspoon of salt in a glass of warm water, and don’t ask children to gargle.

      When is a sore throat serious?

      When you have difficulty breathing or swallowing, drooling, one-sided swelling at the back of the mouth, or a sore throat while taking carbimazole. Get urgent help.
