      Enlarged prostate and the PSA test: symptoms, treatment and prostate cancer screening in Malaysia

      The prostate is a small gland below the bladder that tends to grow with age. An enlarged prostate (benign prostatic hyperplasia, or BPH) isn’t cancer, but it can make passing urine difficult. In Malaysia, about 1 in 6 men aged 40 and over has troublesome symptoms, rising to 1 in 3 at 75 and over. Prostate cancer can cause similar symptoms, and is the third most common cancer in Malaysian men.

      Key points
      • Enlarged prostate symptoms include a weak or stop-start stream, straining, dribbling, needing to go often or urgently, and getting up at night.
      • Being suddenly unable to pass urine is an emergency. Blood in the urine should be checked promptly.
      • Mild symptoms can be managed with lifestyle changes. Alpha-blockers work within days, and 5-alpha reductase inhibitors shrink the prostate over 6 months or more.
      • The PSA blood test can pick up prostate cancer early, but a raised PSA doesn’t always mean cancer, and screening has benefits and harms. Men from 50, or 45 with a family history, should discuss it with their doctor.
      • Prostate cancer is the third most common cancer in Malaysian men, and 63% of cases with staging are found late.

      What is an enlarged prostate?

      The prostate sits just below the bladder, around the tube that carries urine out (the urethra). As men age, it often grows and squeezes the urethra, which makes it harder to pass urine. This is called benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH). Benign means it isn’t cancer.

      In Malaysia: the National Health and Morbidity Survey 2019 found that 16.3% of men aged 40 and over had probable BPH with moderate to severe symptoms, rising from 10.5% at 40 to 44 to 33.4% at 75 and over.

      Symptoms

      • difficulty starting to pass urine, and straining
      • a weak or stop-start stream
      • dribbling at the end
      • a feeling that the bladder hasn’t emptied (kencing tak lawas)
      • needing to go often or urgently
      • getting up at night to pass urine (kencing malam)

      Getting up at night has other causes too, such as drinking late in the evening, alcohol, caffeine, diuretics, diabetes, heart failure and sleep apnoea. See also our guide to urinary tract infections.

      Go to an emergency department if you can’t pass urine at all, especially with lower tummy pain. This is acute urinary retention, and needs a catheter. Blood in the urine, fever with urinary symptoms, or side or back pain also need prompt attention. Untreated BPH can lead to infections, bladder stones and kidney damage.

      How it is checked

      A doctor will ask about your symptoms, sometimes using a short score (the International Prostate Symptom Score) and a 3-day bladder diary, and may do:

      • a rectal examination with a gloved finger to feel the prostate
      • a urine test for infection and blood
      • blood tests for kidney function and PSA, if needed
      • a urine flow test and an ultrasound of the bladder after passing urine

      Treatment

      Lifestyle changes, for mild symptoms:

      • Drink less in the 2 hours before bed, but don’t cut down fluids too much overall.
      • Limit caffeine and alcohol.
      • Try “double voiding”: pass urine, wait a moment, then try again.
      • Treat constipation. See our guide to constipation.
      • Avoid cold remedies containing decongestants or antihistamines, which can make symptoms worse.

      Surgery and procedures: for severe symptoms, or complications such as retention. Transurethral resection of the prostate (TURP) is the standard operation, removing the central part of the prostate through the urethra. Other options include laser treatment, water vapour therapy and microwave treatment, some of which are offered in Malaysian private hospitals. After TURP, most men have dry orgasms (retrograde ejaculation), and some have new erection problems. Ask your surgeon about the risks of each option.

      The PSA test

      PSA (prostate-specific antigen) is a protein made by the prostate, measured with a blood test. A raised level can mean prostate cancer, but also BPH, a urine infection or inflammation. It isn’t a cancer test by itself.

      Before the test, for 48 hours, avoid: ejaculation, vigorous exercise, and long cycling. Wait 4 to 6 weeks after a urine infection. You can eat normally.

      What screening can and can’t do:

      • The large European ERSPC trial found that, after 23 years, PSA screening cut prostate cancer deaths by 13%. About 1 death was prevented for every 456 men invited.
      • But many men with a raised PSA don’t have cancer: only about a quarter had it on biopsy. Screening can also find slow-growing cancers that would never cause harm, leading to unnecessary treatment.
      • That is why guidelines recommend an informed choice, not automatic testing. The US task force advises men aged 55 to 69 to decide individually, and not to screen from 70.
      • European urology guidelines suggest offering a test to informed men from 50, or from 45 with a family history. Some Malaysian urologists recommend yearly tests from these ages.

      If your PSA is raised, it may be repeated, and you may have an MRI before deciding on a biopsy.

      Prostate cancer in Malaysia

      • It is the third most common cancer in Malaysian men, with 6,441 cases recorded from 2017 to 2021, up from 4,189 in the previous five years, according to the National Cancer Registry.
      • Among cases with staging, 63.3% were found at a late stage.
      • Lifetime risk is highest in Chinese men (1 in 50), then Indian (1 in 83) and Malay men (1 in 125).
      • Early prostate cancer often causes no symptoms. Advanced cancer can cause bone pain, especially in the back, along with weight loss and tiredness.
      • Not every prostate cancer needs treatment straight away. Low-risk cancers can be watched closely with PSA tests and MRI scans (active surveillance), with treatment if they grow.

      The PERKESO free health screening doesn’t appear to include PSA as a standard test, so check what your package covers. See our guide to free health screening.

      Where to get checked

      A GP can assess urinary symptoms, test your urine, arrange a PSA test after discussing it with you, start treatment and refer you to a urologist. Our directory lists GP clinics across Kuala Lumpur, Selangor and other states, including clinics open late and at weekends.

      Frequently asked questions

      What are the symptoms of an enlarged prostate?

      A weak or stop-start stream, straining, dribbling, needing to go often or urgently, and getting up at night to pass urine.

      Is an enlarged prostate cancer?

      No, BPH isn’t cancer. But prostate cancer can cause similar symptoms, so it should be checked.

      What should I do if I can’t pass urine?

      Go to an emergency department. Sudden inability to pass urine needs a catheter.

      What is a normal PSA level?

      It varies with age, and there is no single safe number. A doctor interprets it alongside your age, examination and other factors.

      At what age should men have a PSA test?

      European guidelines suggest discussing it from 50, or from 45 with a family history. It is an informed choice, not an automatic test.

      Does a high PSA mean I have cancer?

      Not necessarily. BPH, infection, recent ejaculation or cycling can raise PSA. Only about a quarter of men with a raised PSA in a large trial had cancer on biopsy.

      Will prostate surgery affect my sex life?

      After TURP, most men have dry orgasms, and some have erection problems. Discuss risks of each option with your surgeon.

      Why do I keep waking up at night to pass urine?

      An enlarged prostate is one cause. Others include evening drinks, caffeine, diabetes, heart failure and sleep apnoea.
