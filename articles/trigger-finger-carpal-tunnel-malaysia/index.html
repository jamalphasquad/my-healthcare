      Trigger finger, carpal tunnel and other hand pain: symptoms, treatment and when to see a doctor

      Trigger finger and carpal tunnel syndrome are two of the most common hand problems. Trigger finger makes a finger catch or lock when you bend it. Carpal tunnel syndrome squeezes a nerve at the wrist, causing numbness and tingling, often at night. Both are more common in people with diabetes, and both usually respond well to treatment.

      Key points
      • Trigger finger causes a finger or thumb to catch, click or lock when bent, often worse in the morning, with a tender lump at the base of the finger.
      • Carpal tunnel syndrome causes numbness, tingling and pain in the thumb, index, middle and ring fingers, often waking you at night.
      • Both are more common in women aged 40 to 60, in pregnancy and in people with diabetes. Diabetes about doubles the risk of trigger finger.
      • Splints, rest and steroid injections help many people. A short operation under local anaesthetic usually cures both conditions if other treatment fails.
      • Sudden numbness or weakness in one arm, with a drooping face or slurred speech, could be a stroke. Call 999.

      Trigger finger

      Each finger is bent by a tendon that slides through a tunnel of tough bands at the base of the finger. In trigger finger, the tendon or a small lump on it catches on the first band, so the finger gets stuck when you bend it, then clicks or pops straight.

      Symptoms:

      • clicking, catching or locking of a finger or thumb
      • a finger stuck bent that you have to straighten with your other hand
      • pain and a tender lump on the palm, at the base of the finger
      • symptoms that are often worse first thing in the morning

      Who gets it: it is more common in women aged 40 to 60, and in people with diabetes or rheumatoid arthritis. In a large Swedish study, diabetes doubled the risk. Babies and young children can have a different form, “trigger thumb”, which should be seen by a doctor.

      Treatment:

      • Rest and splinting: avoiding activities that cause pain, and a splint that keeps the finger straight, often at night.
      • Steroid injection into the tendon sheath works for about 7 in 10 people, though it can take days to weeks. A second injection sometimes helps. It works less well in people with diabetes, and can raise blood sugar for 10 to 14 days in those on insulin, so check your levels.
      • Surgery (trigger finger release): a short day-case operation under local anaesthetic to widen the tight band. You can use the hand lightly the same day. The wound heals in a few weeks, though stiffness can take a few months to settle. The problem rarely comes back. A needle release is an alternative some surgeons offer.

      Carpal tunnel syndrome (kebas tangan)

      The median nerve passes through a narrow tunnel in the wrist. In carpal tunnel syndrome, it gets squeezed.

      Symptoms:

      • numbness, tingling or pins and needles in the thumb, index, middle and ring fingers, but not usually the little finger
      • pain or aching in the hand or wrist
      • symptoms that are worse at night and may wake you, often eased by shaking the hand
      • a weak grip, clumsiness or dropping things, and in severe cases wasting of the muscle at the base of the thumb

      Risk factors: pregnancy, being overweight, diabetes, an underactive thyroid, rheumatoid arthritis, repetitive gripping or wrist bending, and using vibrating tools. In a Malaysian hospital study of patients having surgery, 8 in 10 were women, the average age was 53, and nearly a third had diabetes. See our guide to thyroid problems.

      Diagnosis is usually made by examination. A nerve conduction test may be done if the diagnosis is unclear or surgery is being considered.

      Treatment:

      • Wrist splint at night to keep the wrist straight. It can take up to 6 weeks to help.
      • Steroid injection can relieve symptoms, though often for a limited time.
      • Surgery (carpal tunnel release): about 20 minutes under local anaesthetic, usually without an overnight stay. Night symptoms often ease within a week, most people return to normal activities in about a month, and grip strength returns over 2 to 3 months. Surgery is recommended sooner for constant numbness or muscle wasting, to prevent permanent nerve damage.
      • In pregnancy, it often settles within months of the birth.

      De Quervain’s tenosynovitis (thumb and wrist pain)

      De Quervain’s causes pain on the thumb side of the wrist, worse when gripping, lifting or twisting, from inflamed tendons. It is common in women in their 40s and 50s, and in new mothers, often starting a month or two after the birth, partly from lifting the baby.

      A doctor may test it by asking you to tuck your thumb into your fist and bend your wrist towards the little finger, which causes pain. Treatment includes a thumb splint, ice, anti-inflammatory painkillers and a steroid injection. Surgery is an option if it doesn’t improve.

      Other causes of hand numbness

      • A trapped nerve in the neck, causing neck pain running down the arm with tingling or weakness. See our guide to slipped discs.
      • The ulnar nerve at the elbow, causing numbness in the ring and little fingers, worse with the elbow bent. See a doctor if it lasts more than 6 weeks, as muscle weakness can become permanent.
      • Diabetes, which can damage nerves in the hands and feet.
      • Low vitamin B12. See our guide to anaemia.

      Call 999 straight away for sudden numbness or weakness in an arm or leg, especially on one side, with a drooping face, slurred speech, loss of balance or vision changes, even if it passes. These are signs of a stroke. See our guide to stroke and heart attack.

      Check your blood sugar

      Hand problems such as trigger finger, carpal tunnel syndrome and frozen shoulder are all more common with diabetes. In Malaysia, 15.6% of adults have diabetes, and 2 in 5 of them don’t know it. If you develop any of these, ask for a blood sugar test. See our guide to normal blood sugar levels.

      When to see a doctor

      • a finger that keeps locking, or that you can’t straighten
      • numbness or tingling that keeps coming back, wakes you at night, or doesn’t improve with a splint
      • weakness, clumsiness or muscle wasting in your hand
      • a hot, swollen, red finger or hand, which could be an infection

      Where to get your hand checked

      A GP can examine your hand, check your blood sugar, give a steroid injection in some cases, and refer you to an orthopaedic or hand surgeon or for a nerve test. Our directory lists GP clinics across Kuala Lumpur, Selangor and other states, including clinics open on Saturdays.

      Frequently asked questions

      What causes trigger finger?

      A tendon or a small lump on it catches on a tight band at the base of the finger. It is more common in women aged 40 to 60 and in people with diabetes or rheumatoid arthritis.

      Can trigger finger go away on its own?

      Sometimes, especially with rest and a splint. Many people need a steroid injection, and some need a short operation.

      How effective is a steroid injection for trigger finger?

      It works for about 7 in 10 people, but less well in people with diabetes. A second injection is sometimes given.

      What are the symptoms of carpal tunnel syndrome?

      Numbness, tingling and pain in the thumb, index, middle and ring fingers, often worse at night, sometimes with a weak grip.

      Why do my hands go numb at night?

      Carpal tunnel syndrome is a common cause, as the wrist often bends during sleep. A night splint can help.

      How long does it take to recover from carpal tunnel surgery?

      Most people return to normal activities in about a month, and grip strength returns over 2 to 3 months.

      What is De Quervain’s?

      Painful, inflamed tendons on the thumb side of the wrist, common in new mothers and in women in their 40s and 50s.

      When is hand numbness an emergency?

      When it is sudden and comes with weakness, a drooping face, slurred speech or loss of balance, which may be a stroke. Call 999.
