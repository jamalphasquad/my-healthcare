      Bartholin cyst, ovarian cysts and fibroids: symptoms, treatment and when to worry

      Lumps and cysts are common in women and are usually harmless. A Bartholin cyst is a swelling at the entrance to the vagina. Ovarian cysts are fluid-filled sacs on the ovaries, and most disappear on their own. Fibroids are non-cancerous growths in the womb. Here is how to tell them apart, what treatment involves, and when to get help quickly.

      Key points
      • About 2 in 100 women get a Bartholin cyst. It is usually a painless lump on one side of the vaginal opening. If it becomes infected (an abscess), it gets very painful, red and swollen.
      • Warm baths can help a small cyst drain. An abscess needs a doctor, who may drain it or fit a small catheter. Women over 40 with a new vulval lump should have it checked for cancer.
      • Most ovarian cysts are “functional” cysts linked to the menstrual cycle and disappear within a few months. Larger, persistent or post-menopausal cysts need monitoring.
      • Sudden, severe pelvic pain with vomiting, dizziness or fainting can mean a twisted or burst cyst. Go to an emergency department.
      • Fibroids affect up to 8 in 10 women by age 50. Many cause no symptoms. Treatment depends on symptoms such as heavy periods, and fibroids almost never become cancer.

      Bartholin cyst

      The two Bartholin glands are pea-sized glands on either side of the vaginal opening that produce lubricating fluid. If a gland’s opening becomes blocked, fluid builds up and forms a cyst, usually on one side. It is most common between the ages of 20 and 50.

      • Cyst: a soft, usually painless lump near the vaginal opening, from pea to golf-ball size. It may be uncomfortable when walking, sitting or having sex.
      • Abscess: if the cyst becomes infected, it becomes very painful, red, hot and swollen, sometimes with fever or pus. It is usually caused by common skin or gut bacteria, and only occasionally by an STI such as gonorrhoea or chlamydia. See our guide to STI testing.

      Home care: sit in a few inches of warm water (a sitz bath) several times a day for 3 to 4 days, or use a warm compress. This may help a small cyst drain. Take paracetamol or ibuprofen for pain. Don’t squeeze or try to burst it.

      Treatment by a doctor:

      • Antibiotics, if the cyst is infected or there is an STI
      • Catheter drainage: after a small cut under local anaesthetic, a tiny balloon catheter is left in for about 4 weeks so a new opening forms
      • Marsupialisation: a minor operation that creates a permanent opening, often used for cysts that keep coming back

      Cysts can come back after any treatment, about 1 in 10 after marsupialisation and more often after simple drainage. If you are over 40, a new vulval lump should be checked, and a small sample (biopsy) may be taken, as Bartholin gland cancer, although rare, needs ruling out.

      Ovarian cysts (cyst ovari)

      Ovarian cysts are fluid-filled sacs on or in an ovary. They are very common and usually harmless.

      • Functional cysts are the most common and are linked to the menstrual cycle: a follicle that doesn’t release its egg, or the sac left after ovulation filling with fluid. They usually disappear within a few weeks to 3 months.
      • Other types include dermoid cysts (which can contain hair or skin tissue), endometriomas or “chocolate cysts” caused by endometriosis (see our guide to period pain and endometriosis), and cystadenomas.
      • PCOS is different: the “cysts” are many small follicles, not true cysts. See our PCOS (PMOS) guide.

      Symptoms: often none. Cysts are frequently found by chance on a scan. Some cause pelvic pain, bloating, pain during sex, needing to pass urine often, period changes or feeling full quickly.

      Monitoring and treatment: a cyst is usually checked with an ultrasound, often repeated a few weeks or months later to see if it has gone. UK guidance suggests small simple cysts under 5 cm in women who still have periods usually need no follow-up. Larger, persistent or complex cysts may need an MRI, blood tests or keyhole surgery to remove them. After the menopause, any cyst should be checked more carefully, with repeat scans and blood tests.

      When pelvic pain is an emergency

      Go to an emergency department or call 999 for:

      • sudden, severe pain in the lower tummy or pelvis, especially on one side, with vomiting, dizziness or fainting. An ovarian cyst may have twisted the ovary (torsion) or burst.
      • pelvic pain with fever, fast breathing or cold, clammy skin
      • very heavy vaginal bleeding
      • severe pain or fainting if you could be pregnant, which may be an ectopic pregnancy. See our guide to early pregnancy warning signs.

      See a doctor soon for a Bartholin lump that becomes painful, red or swollen over hours or days, or comes with fever or pus.

      Ovarian cancer warning signs

      Most ovarian cysts aren’t cancer. But ovarian cancer is the fifth most common cancer in Malaysian women, and there is no screening test for it. Malaysian gynaecologists report that about three-quarters of cases are found at an advanced stage.

      See a doctor if you have any of these on most days, roughly 12 or more times a month:

      • persistent bloating, or a swollen tummy
      • pain in the tummy or pelvis
      • feeling full quickly, or loss of appetite
      • needing to pass urine more urgently or often

      These symptoms are usually caused by something less serious, but they are worth checking, especially if they are new. Regular Pap smears and HPV tests check the cervix, not the ovaries.

      Fibroids

      Fibroids are non-cancerous growths of muscle in or on the womb. They are very common, affecting between 2 and 8 in 10 women by age 50, and are more common in women of Asian or African background, with obesity or with a family history.

      Symptoms: many women have none. Others have:

      • heavy or painful periods, which can lead to iron-deficiency anaemia
      • pressure or a swollen tummy
      • needing to pass urine often, or constipation
      • pain during sex
      • less often, difficulty getting pregnant or miscarriage

      Treatment depends on your symptoms, the size and position of the fibroids, and whether you want children:

      • No treatment if they cause no problems. Fibroids usually shrink after the menopause.
      • Medicines: tranexamic acid and anti-inflammatory painkillers for heavy periods, hormonal contraception or a hormonal coil (see our contraception guide), or injections that shrink fibroids temporarily.
      • Procedures and surgery: removing the fibroids while keeping the womb (myomectomy), blocking their blood supply (uterine artery embolisation), or removing the womb (hysterectomy). Some private hospitals in Malaysia also offer focused ultrasound (HIFU) for selected patients.

      Fibroids don’t turn into cancer. A rare cancer of the womb muscle, leiomyosarcoma, affects fewer than 1 in 1,000 women thought to have fibroids.

      Where to get checked

      A GP can examine a Bartholin lump, treat an infection, arrange a pelvic ultrasound and refer you to a gynaecologist. Many clinics can arrange for a female doctor if you ask. Our directory lists GP clinics across Kuala Lumpur, Selangor and other states, including clinics open late.

      Frequently asked questions

      What is a Bartholin cyst?

      A fluid-filled swelling of one of the Bartholin glands, which sit either side of the vaginal opening. It is usually painless unless it becomes infected.

      Will a Bartholin cyst go away on its own?

      Small cysts often do, and warm baths can help them drain. A painful or infected cyst (abscess) needs a doctor.

      Is a Bartholin cyst an STI?

      Usually not. Most infections are caused by common bacteria, although an STI is occasionally involved, so testing may be offered.

      Are ovarian cysts dangerous?

      Most are harmless and disappear within a few months. Sudden severe pain with vomiting or fainting needs emergency care, as a cyst may have twisted or burst.

      Do ovarian cysts need surgery?

      Only some do, such as large, persistent or unusual cysts. Most are simply rechecked with an ultrasound.

      What are the symptoms of fibroids?

      Heavy or painful periods, pressure in the tummy, needing to pass urine often and pain during sex. Many women have no symptoms.

      Can fibroids turn into cancer?

      No. Fibroids aren’t cancer and don’t turn into it. A rare cancer of the womb muscle can look similar, but it is very uncommon.

      What are the warning signs of ovarian cancer?

      Persistent bloating, pelvic or tummy pain, feeling full quickly and needing to pass urine more often, on most days. See a doctor if these are new.
