      Bowel cancer in Malaysia: symptoms, stool test screening and colonoscopy

      Bowel cancer, also called colorectal or colon cancer (kanser usus), is now the most common cancer in Malaysian men and the second most common in women. Three in four cases are found only at a late stage, even though a simple home stool test can pick it up early, when it is far easier to treat. This guide explains the symptoms, who should be screened and how to get tested in Malaysia.

      Key points
      • Bowel cancer is the most common cancer in Malaysian men and the second most common in women. Cases rose by more than half between 2012–2016 and 2017–2021.
      • About 3 in 4 cases are diagnosed at stage 3 or 4. Early bowel cancer often causes no symptoms.
      • Symptoms include blood in the stool, a change in bowel habit, tummy pain or bloating, weight loss you can’t explain, and tiredness from anaemia.
      • Adults aged 50 to 75 at average risk should do a stool test (iFOBT), which government health clinics offer. If the result is positive, you need a colonoscopy.
      • If a close relative had bowel cancer, colonoscopy screening usually starts at 40, or earlier.

      Bowel cancer in Malaysia

      The Malaysia National Cancer Registry report for 2017–2021 found that:

      • Bowel (colorectal) cancer made up 14.1% of all cancers. It was the second most common cancer overall after breast cancer, and the most common in men.
      • About 23,900 people were diagnosed in those five years, 54% more than in 2012–2016. About 15% of them were under 50.
      • Of the cases with a known stage, about three in four were found at stage 3 or 4. Only about 6% were caught at stage 1.
      • The lifetime risk is about 1 in 44 for men and 1 in 62 for women. It is highest among Chinese Malaysians, at 1 in 30 for men.

      In US data, about 9 in 10 people whose cancer is found before it spreads are alive 5 years later. When it has spread to distant organs, the figure is about 1 in 7.

      Bowel cancer symptoms (kanser usus simptom)

      • Blood in your stool, which may look red or black, or bleeding from the bottom
      • A change in bowel habit that lasts several weeks, such as looser stools, constipation or going more often
      • Feeling you need to go even after you have just been
      • Tummy pain, a lump in the tummy, or bloating
      • Losing weight without trying
      • Tiredness or breathlessness from anaemia (a low blood count)

      Most people with these symptoms don’t have cancer. Haemorrhoids (piles), for example, often cause bleeding. You can’t tell the difference yourself, though, so always get bleeding from the bottom checked by a doctor. Malaysian guidelines advise an urgent colonoscopy within 2 weeks for unexplained rectal bleeding with a change in bowel habit or weight loss, and for iron-deficiency anaemia.

      Early bowel cancer often causes no symptoms at all, so it is worth being screened even if you feel well.

      Who is at higher risk?

      • Being 50 or older, though younger adults can get it too
      • Having a close relative (parent, brother, sister or child) with bowel cancer, or an inherited condition such as Lynch syndrome
      • A history of bowel polyps, or inflammatory bowel disease (Crohn’s disease or ulcerative colitis)
      • Smoking, alcohol, being overweight and being inactive
      • Eating a lot of processed meat (such as sausages, ham and luncheon meat) and red meat, and little fruit and vegetables. The WHO’s cancer agency classifies processed meat as a cause of bowel cancer.

      Bowel cancer screening: the stool test

      Malaysian guidelines recommend that adults aged 50 to 75 at average risk do an immunochemical faecal occult blood test (iFOBT), also called a FIT test. It picks up tiny amounts of blood in the stool that you can’t see.

      Government health clinics (Klinik Kesihatan) offer the test under the Ministry of Health’s national screening programme, which has supplied test kits to participating clinics since 2014. Ask for it at your next visit, and ask whether there is a charge. Selangor’s Selangor Saring programme has also included the test. The National Cancer Society Malaysia offers it for RM20, and private clinics and pharmacies set their own fees.

      You collect a small stool sample at home with the kit and return it to the clinic. You don’t need to change what you eat beforehand.

      The 2017 guidelines recommend testing every year if the result is negative. Some clinics schedule the repeat every 2 years instead.

      If the result is positive, you are referred for a colonoscopy. A positive test doesn’t mean you have cancer, and most positives turn out to be polyps or other causes, but it still has to be followed up.

      Too few Malaysians follow up. Between 2014 and 2018, only about 55% of people with a positive stool test went on to have the colonoscopy they needed. In Selangor’s screening programme, only 15–27% of people with abnormal results followed up. If your test is positive, please go for the colonoscopy.

      If bowel cancer runs in your family

      For people with a family history, Malaysian guidelines recommend colonoscopy instead of the stool test:

      Family historyWhen to start colonoscopy
      A parent, brother, sister or child diagnosed before 60, or any relative before 50At 40, or 10 years younger than the youngest relative was at diagnosis, whichever is earlier. Repeat every 3 to 5 years.
      A parent, brother, sister or child diagnosed at 60 or olderAt 40. Repeat every 10 years.

      Tell your doctor about your family history, including how old your relative was when they were diagnosed.

      What happens during a colonoscopy

      A colonoscopy uses a thin, flexible tube with a camera to look inside the large bowel. It can find cancer early, and polyps found during the test can be removed before they turn into cancer.

      Beforehand, you follow a special diet and take a strong laxative to empty your bowel.

      The test usually takes 30 to 45 minutes. You are usually offered sedation and painkillers. The doctor can remove polyps and take small samples (biopsies).

      If you had sedation, don’t drive for 24 hours afterwards. Slight bleeding for a day or two is common.

      Serious complications are uncommon. In the UK screening programme, a tear in the bowel happens in about 1 in 1,700 colonoscopies.

      Treatment

      Surgery to remove the cancer is the main treatment and gives the best chance of a cure. Chemotherapy and radiotherapy may be used before or after surgery, and newer targeted treatments and immunotherapy help some people with advanced cancer. A team of specialists plans the treatment.

      To lower your risk, stay active, keep to a healthy weight, don’t smoke, limit alcohol, eat less processed and red meat, and eat more fruit and vegetables.

      Where to ask about bowel cancer screening

      Your nearest Klinik Kesihatan can offer the stool test from age 50. Private GP clinics can also arrange a stool test, check symptoms such as bleeding, and refer you for a colonoscopy. Our directory lists GP clinics across Kuala Lumpur, Selangor and other states.

      Frequently asked questions

      What are the symptoms of bowel cancer? (Kanser usus simptom)

      Blood in the stool or bleeding from the bottom, a change in bowel habit that lasts weeks, tummy pain or bloating, weight loss you can’t explain, and tiredness from anaemia. Early bowel cancer often has no symptoms.

      How much does bowel cancer screening cost in Malaysia?

      Government health clinics offer the iFOBT stool test to eligible adults aged 50 to 75 under a national screening programme, so ask your clinic whether there is a charge. The National Cancer Society Malaysia charges RM20, and private clinics set their own fees. If the result is positive, the next step is a colonoscopy.

      How is the iFOBT stool test done?

      You collect a small stool sample at home with the kit and return it to the clinic, where it is tested for hidden blood. You don’t need to change your diet.

      At what age should I start bowel cancer screening?

      Most people start at 50. If a close relative had bowel cancer, colonoscopy usually starts at 40, or 10 years before the age your relative was diagnosed if they were under 60.

      Can bowel cancer affect young people?

      Yes. About 15% of bowel cancers in Malaysia in 2017–2021 were in people under 50. Being young is no reason to ignore symptoms such as bleeding.

      Is bleeding from the bottom always cancer?

      No. Haemorrhoids (piles) and fissures often cause it. You can’t tell the cause yourself, though, so always have rectal bleeding checked by a doctor.
