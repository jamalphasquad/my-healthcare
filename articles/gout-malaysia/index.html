      Gout in Malaysia: symptoms, uric acid levels, foods to limit and treatment

      Gout is one of the most painful kinds of arthritis, and doctors in Malaysia say they are seeing it in younger adults. An attack usually comes on suddenly, often in the big toe at night. Here is what causes it, what your uric acid test means, and the long-term treatment that stops attacks coming back.

      Key points
      • Gout is caused by uric acid crystals in a joint. Attacks usually start suddenly, often at night, with severe pain, redness and swelling, most often in the big toe.
      • A uric acid level above 408 µmol/L (6.8 mg/dL) is high, but a high level alone doesn’t mean gout, and the level can be normal during an attack.
      • Alcohol, sugary drinks, red meat, some seafood and excess weight raise the risk. Some blood pressure medicines can too.
      • Attacks are treated with colchicine, anti-inflammatories or steroids. People with repeated attacks need long-term uric acid-lowering medicine, aiming for below 360 µmol/L.
      • Don’t stop uric acid-lowering medicine during an attack, and never try to drain gout lumps (tophi) yourself.

      What is gout?

      Gout happens when uric acid, a waste product the body makes when it breaks down substances called purines, builds up in the blood and forms needle-like crystals in a joint. The immune system attacks the crystals, causing sudden, intense inflammation.

      There are no national figures on how common gout is in Malaysia. Hospital studies show it usually starts between the ages of 30 and 60 and mostly affects men. Specialists say they are seeing more young adults with gout, linked to rising obesity, and nearly half of Malaysians are overweight.

      Gout symptoms: what an attack feels like

      • Sudden, severe pain in one joint, often starting at night and peaking within 24 hours. Many people describe it as throbbing, burning or like being stabbed.
      • The joint becomes red, hot and swollen, and too painful to touch, even with a bedsheet.
      • The big toe is the most common site, followed by the midfoot and ankle. Knees, wrists, hands and elbows can also be affected.
      • Some people have a fever during an attack.

      Attacks usually settle within a week or two, but without treatment they tend to come back more often and affect more joints. Over time, firm white or yellow lumps called tophi can form under the skin, at the toes, ears, elbows and fingers.

      Don’t drain gout lumps yourself

      Puncturing a tophus at home can cause a serious, even life-threatening, infection. See a doctor if a lump is painful, open or leaking. Also see a doctor urgently for a hot, swollen joint with a high fever, as it could be a joint infection.

      What your uric acid level means

      Uric acid levelWhat it means
      Above 408 µmol/L (6.8 mg/dL)High (hyperuricaemia): crystals can start to form
      Below 360 µmol/L (6 mg/dL)Treatment target for people on uric acid-lowering medicine
      Below 300 µmol/L (5 mg/dL)Target for severe gout, such as with tophi or frequent attacks

      Reference ranges vary slightly between laboratories, so check the range printed on your report.

      • A high level isn’t the same as gout. Many people with high uric acid never get an attack, and Malaysian guidelines don’t recommend treating high uric acid without symptoms.
      • The level can be normal during an attack. Doctors may repeat the test two or more weeks after the attack settles.

      What causes gout, and foods to limit

      Things that raise the risk of gout, according to the Ministry of Health’s gout guidelines:

      • Being overweight
      • Alcohol, including beer
      • Sugar-sweetened drinks and fruit juice
      • Red meat, organ meats and some seafood, such as anchovies, sardines and shellfish
      • Some medicines, including diuretics (“water pills”) and some blood pressure medicines
      • Kidney disease, diabetes, a family history of gout, and being male or past menopause

      Foods that may help: dairy products are linked to a lower risk, and purines from plant foods such as beans and vegetables don’t raise it. Cherries may lower the chance of attacks, but the evidence is limited. Losing weight, drinking enough water and cutting alcohol and sweet drinks all help.

      Don’t stop any prescribed medicine, including low-dose aspirin for your heart, without talking to your doctor. They can switch blood pressure medicines if needed.

      Treating gout

      During an attack: your doctor may prescribe colchicine, an anti-inflammatory painkiller (NSAID), or a steroid. Rest the joint, raise it, and apply an ice pack wrapped in a towel for up to 20 minutes. Start treatment as early as you can.

      Preventing attacks: painkillers treat the pain, but not the cause. People with repeated attacks, tophi or kidney stones usually need long-term uric acid-lowering medicine, most often allopurinol, to bring the level below the target.

      • It is started at a low dose and increased slowly, with regular blood tests.
      • Doctors often add a low-dose anti-inflammatory for the first 3 to 6 months, as attacks can briefly increase when treatment starts.
      • Keep taking it during an attack, and long term. Stopping is the most common reason treatment fails. In one Malaysian clinic, only about a third of patients reached their target.
      • Rarely, allopurinol causes a serious skin reaction. Stop it and see a doctor at once if you get a rash, blisters or mouth sores.

      Gout or pseudogout?

      Pseudogout (calcium pyrophosphate deposition disease) causes similar sudden, painful, swollen joints, but it is caused by calcium crystals, not uric acid. It is more common in people over 60, most often affects the knee, and attacks last 1 to 2 weeks. It doesn’t respond to uric acid-lowering medicine. A doctor can tell them apart by testing fluid from the joint, or from X-ray findings.

      Why gout needs long-term care

      Untreated gout can lead to permanent joint damage, tophi, kidney stones and kidney disease. People with gout also have higher rates of heart disease, diabetes and high blood pressure, so a check of your blood pressure, blood sugar, cholesterol and kidney function is part of good gout care.

      Where to get gout treated

      Most gout is diagnosed and managed by GPs, who can treat attacks, check your uric acid and kidney function, and start long-term treatment. Our directory lists GP clinics across Kuala Lumpur, Selangor and other states, including clinics open late and at weekends.

      Frequently asked questions

      What are the symptoms of gout?

      Sudden, severe pain in a joint, often the big toe, usually starting at night, with redness, heat and swelling. The joint may be too painful to touch. Some people also have a fever.

      What uric acid level causes gout?

      Uric acid above 408 µmol/L (6.8 mg/dL) is high enough for crystals to form, but many people with high levels never get gout. On treatment, the target is below 360 µmol/L (6 mg/dL).

      What causes gout in the foot? (Punca gout di kaki)

      Uric acid crystals collect in the joints of the feet, especially the big toe. Risk rises with excess weight, alcohol, sugary drinks, red meat, some seafood, some medicines and kidney disease.

      What is the difference between gout and pseudogout?

      Gout is caused by uric acid crystals and often affects the big toe. Pseudogout is caused by calcium crystals, is more common over 60, and usually affects the knee.

      Should I stop allopurinol during a gout attack?

      No. Keep taking it as prescribed. Stopping and starting can trigger more attacks. Your doctor will treat the attack separately.

      Can gout be cured?

      Gout can be controlled. Keeping uric acid below the target with long-term medicine and lifestyle changes can stop attacks and dissolve tophi over time.
