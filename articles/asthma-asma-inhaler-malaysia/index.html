      Asma (asthma) in Malaysia: inhalers, what to do in an attack, and keeping it under control

      Asthma (asma, often called lelah) is a long-term condition in which the airways become inflamed and narrow, causing wheezing, coughing, chest tightness and breathlessness. With the right inhalers used the right way, most people can keep it under control. Malaysian and international guidelines now agree that relying on a blue reliever inhaler alone is risky.

      Key points
      • The 2023 National Health and Morbidity Survey found more than 1.4 million Malaysian adults have diagnosed asthma, and about 3 in 10 people with asthma don’t know they have it.
      • Malaysia’s 2024 adult guideline says a reliever (SABA, such as salbutamol) shouldn’t be used on its own. Everyone with asthma should have treatment that includes an inhaled steroid.
      • Using a reliever more than twice a week, or waking at night with asthma, is a sign that your asthma isn’t controlled. See your doctor.
      • Inhaler technique matters. Shake the inhaler, breathe out fully, press as you start to breathe in, use a spacer, and rinse your mouth after a steroid inhaler.
      • In an attack, sit up and use your reliever as your action plan says. Call 999 if you aren’t improving, are getting worse, or can’t speak in sentences.

      What is asthma?

      In asthma, the airways in the lungs are sensitive and inflamed. When they react to a trigger, they tighten and produce extra mucus, making it hard to breathe.

      Symptoms come and go, and are often worse at night or during or after exercise:

      • wheezing (a whistling sound when breathing)
      • coughing, especially at night
      • chest tightness
      • shortness of breath (semput or sesak nafas)

      Common triggers in Malaysia: colds and other viral infections, house dust mites, pets, haze, smoke, vaping, strong smells such as perfume and cleaning products, exhaust fumes, cold air (including air-conditioning) and exercise.

      In Malaysia: more than 1.4 million adults have diagnosed asthma, and almost half a million children aged 6 to 17. Among children with asthma, 1 in 2 had at least one attack in the past year.

      How asthma is diagnosed

      Your doctor will ask about your symptoms and listen to your chest. The preferred test is spirometry, where you blow hard into a machine that measures how much and how fast you can breathe out. If your breathing improves by a set amount after a reliever inhaler, that supports asthma.

      A peak flow meter can also help. Readings that vary a lot from day to day, or improve with treatment, point to asthma. Asthma can be missed, so see a doctor if you often cough at night, wheeze or get breathless with colds.

      Reliever and preventer inhalers

      TypeWhat it doesGeneric names
      Reliever (SABA)Opens the airways within minutes. Doesn’t treat the inflammation.Salbutamol, terbutaline
      Preventer (inhaled steroid, ICS)Calms the inflammation over time, so attacks are less likely. Must be used every day, even when well.Budesonide, beclometasone, fluticasone, ciclesonide
      Combination (ICS plus long-acting reliever)A preventer and a long-acting airway opener in one inhaler. Some can also be used as a reliever.Budesonide-formoterol, beclometasone-formoterol, fluticasone-salmeterol

      Why a blue reliever alone isn’t enough: the Global Initiative for Asthma no longer recommends treating asthma with a short-acting reliever alone, because it raises the risk of severe attacks and death. Using 3 or more reliever canisters a year raises the risk of severe attacks. Malaysia’s 2024 adult guideline agrees that a reliever shouldn’t be used alone and that everyone with asthma should have treatment containing an inhaled steroid.

      Many doctors now prescribe a single budesonide-formoterol or beclometasone-formoterol inhaler used both as a daily preventer and as a reliever. Others prescribe a daily preventer with a separate reliever. Follow the plan your doctor gives you, and don’t change your inhalers without advice.

      How to use your inhaler properly

      Many people use their inhaler incorrectly. Malaysia’s guideline lists the most common mistakes: not breathing out fully first, pressing the canister before breathing in, breathing out into the device, not holding it upright, and not holding the breath for long enough.

      Metered-dose inhaler (spray):

      1. Shake the inhaler and remove the cap.
      2. Breathe out fully, away from the inhaler.
      3. Seal your lips around the mouthpiece, or the spacer.
      4. Press the canister once as you start to breathe in slowly and deeply.
      5. Hold your breath for up to 10 seconds, or as long as is comfortable.
      6. Wait about 30 seconds before a second puff.
      • Use a spacer, especially with a steroid inhaler. It helps more medicine reach the lungs. If you don’t have one, a homemade spacer made from a plastic bottle can be used in an emergency, although it isn’t as effective.
      • Dry powder inhalers need a fast, deep breath in, and shouldn’t be shaken or breathed into.
      • Rinse your mouth with water and spit after using a steroid inhaler, to reduce the risk of a sore mouth, hoarse voice and thrush.
      • Is it empty? Check the dose counter, or count the doses used. Shaking can be misleading, because propellant remains after the medicine runs out.

      Ask your doctor, nurse or pharmacist to check your technique at every review.

      What to do in an asthma attack

      Signs of an attack: your reliever isn’t helping or you need it more and more, you are wheezing, coughing or very breathless, or you can’t speak, eat or sleep normally.

      If you use a blue reliever inhaler (UK guidance):

      1. Sit up straight and try to stay calm.
      2. Take 1 puff of your reliever every 30 to 60 seconds, up to 10 puffs, using a spacer if you have one.
      3. Call 999 if you feel worse at any point, or don’t feel better after 10 puffs.
      4. If the ambulance hasn’t arrived after 10 minutes, repeat step 2.

      If you use a combined preventer-and-reliever inhaler (such as budesonide-formoterol), follow your action plan. UK guidance is 1 puff every 1 to 3 minutes, up to 6 puffs, then call 999 if you aren’t better.

      Call 999 straight away if the person can only speak in single words, is too breathless to talk, has blue or grey lips, or is drowsy or confused.

      Even if you recover at home, see a doctor the same day, as an attack means your treatment needs reviewing.

      Is your asthma under control?

      Over the past 4 weeks, have you:

      • had daytime symptoms more than twice a week?
      • woken at night because of your asthma?
      • needed your reliever more than twice a week?
      • had to limit your activities because of your asthma?

      If you answered yes to any of these, your asthma may not be well controlled. See your doctor. Ask for a written asthma action plan that tells you what to take each day, how to spot when you are getting worse, and what to do in an attack. Malaysia’s guideline recommends a review every 3 to 6 months.

      • Haze: on days when the Air Pollutant Index is unhealthy, stay indoors where possible, keep taking your medicines and see a doctor promptly if your asthma gets worse. See our haze guide.
      • Smoking and vaping make asthma worse and increase the risk of attacks.
      • Flu and pneumococcal vaccines may be recommended. See our guides to the flu vaccine and pneumococcal vaccine.

      Children, pregnancy and common myths

      • Children under 6 should use a spacer with a face mask, and older children a spacer with a mouthpiece. The 2024 Malaysian children’s consensus discourages nebulisers at home. Give a copy of the action plan to your child’s school or nursery.
      • Growth: low to moderate doses of inhaled steroids are considered safe for children. Children on high doses need their growth monitored.
      • Pregnancy: keep taking your preventer. Uncontrolled asthma is a greater risk to you and your baby than the inhaler.
      • “Inhalers are addictive.” They aren’t. Using them correctly keeps asthma under control.
      • “Steroid inhalers are dangerous.” Used as recommended, inhaled steroids are unlikely to cause lasting side effects, because only a small dose reaches the rest of the body.
      • “I only need my inhaler when I’m ill.” A preventer works only if it is used every day, even when you feel well.

      Frequently asked questions

      What is asma in English?

      Asthma. Many Malaysians also call it lelah, although lelah can also mean tiredness.

      What is the difference between a reliever and a preventer inhaler?

      A reliever (such as salbutamol) opens the airways quickly during symptoms. A preventer (an inhaled steroid) calms the inflammation over time and must be used every day.

      Can I use only a blue inhaler for asthma?

      Current Malaysian and international guidelines say a short-acting reliever shouldn’t be used on its own. Treatment should include an inhaled steroid. Ask your doctor.

      What should I do in an asthma attack?

      Sit up and take your reliever as your action plan says. With a blue reliever, UK guidance is 1 puff every 30 to 60 seconds up to 10 puffs. Call 999 if you are not improving or are getting worse.

      Are steroid inhalers safe?

      Yes, when used as recommended. Only a small dose reaches the rest of the body. Rinse your mouth afterwards to prevent a sore mouth or thrush.

      Can asthma go away?

      Symptoms can improve, and some children grow out of them. Asthma can return, so keep your action plan and get symptoms checked if they come back.

      How do I know if my inhaler is empty?

      Check the dose counter, or count the puffs used. Shaking it can be misleading.
