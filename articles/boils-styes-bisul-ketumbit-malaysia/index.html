      Boils (bisul) and styes (ketumbit): causes, home care, draining, and when to see a doctor

      A boil (bisul) is a painful, pus-filled lump that forms when a hair follicle gets infected. A stye (ketumbit) is a similar small infection on the edge of the eyelid. Both are usually caused by Staphylococcus bacteria, and most heal on their own within one to two weeks with warm compresses. Some need a doctor.

      Key points
      • Boils and styes are caused by Staphylococcus aureus bacteria, which about 1 in 5 people carry harmlessly in the nose and on the skin.
      • A warm, clean compress for about 10 minutes several times a day helps a boil or stye come to a head and drain. Most boils heal in about 10 days, and most styes within a week.
      • Don’t squeeze, pierce or cut a boil or stye. It can push the infection deeper, especially on the face.
      • See a doctor for a boil on the face, a large or very painful boil, fever, spreading redness, a cluster of boils (carbuncle), or a stye that affects your vision or causes eyelid swelling.
      • Boils that keep coming back can be a sign of undiagnosed diabetes. About 2 in 5 Malaysian adults with diabetes don’t know they have it.

      What is a boil (bisul)?

      A boil (furuncle) is a deep infection of a hair follicle. It starts as a tender red lump, which swells and fills with pus over a few days, and usually forms a yellow or white head before bursting and draining. A carbuncle is a cluster of boils joined together under the skin, often on the back of the neck, shoulders or thighs. It is more likely to cause fever and needs a doctor.

      Cause: the bacterium Staphylococcus aureus. About 10 to 20% of people carry it, mostly in the nose, armpits and groin. It gets into the skin through small cuts, grazes, insect bites, or friction from tight clothing and sweat.

      More likely if you have diabetes, are overweight, have eczema, a weak immune system or iron deficiency, smoke, or live with someone who gets boils.

      Caring for a boil at home

      • Warm compress: hold a clean cloth soaked in warm (not hot) water on the boil for about 10 minutes, up to 4 times a day. This eases the pain and helps it come to a head and drain on its own.
      • Don’t squeeze, pick or pierce it, and don’t cut it open yourself. This can push the infection deeper or spread it.
      • Once it drains, wash gently with soap and water, and cover it with a clean dressing until it heals.
      • Wash your hands after touching it. Use your own towel, and wash towels, bedding and clothes on a hot wash.
      • Take paracetamol or ibuprofen for pain if needed.
      • Avoid swimming pools and shared gym equipment until it has healed.

      Most boils heal within about 10 days.

      When to see a doctor about a boil

      See a doctor the same day if:

      • the boil is on your face, especially around the nose and upper lip. Veins here connect to the inside of the skull, so infection can rarely spread there, and squeezing makes it more likely.
      • the skin around it is red, hot, swollen and spreading (cellulitis)
      • you have a fever, chills or feel unwell
      • it is very large or very painful, or you have a carbuncle
      • you have diabetes or a weak immune system

      See a GP if a boil hasn’t got better after 2 weeks, or you keep getting boils.

      Treatment: under the Ministry of Health’s antimicrobial guideline, the main treatment for a boil or abscess full of pus is drainage: the doctor numbs the skin, makes a small cut to let the pus out, and dresses the wound. Antibiotics aren’t needed for most simple boils, but are used for large or spreading infections, carbuncles, fever, facial boils, or if drainage alone hasn’t worked. The doctor may send a swab to check which antibiotics work, as some staph bacteria (MRSA) are resistant to common ones.

      Boils that keep coming back

      • Check your blood sugar. Poorly controlled diabetes makes boils more common and slower to heal. In Malaysia, 15.6% of adults have diabetes and about 2 in 5 of them don’t know it. See our guide to normal blood sugar levels.
      • Clearing the bacteria: for repeated boils, a doctor may suggest an antiseptic nasal ointment and an antiseptic body wash for a few days, sometimes for everyone in the household, along with washing towels and bedding.
      • Painful lumps that keep coming back in the armpits, groin or under the breasts, with blackheads and scarring, may be hidradenitis suppurativa, a long-term inflammatory skin condition rather than ordinary boils. It needs a doctor or dermatologist.

      Styes (ketumbit) and chalazion

      • A stye (hordeolum, ketumbit) is a painful red lump on the edge of the eyelid, caused by bacteria infecting an eyelash follicle or eyelid gland. It usually goes away within a week.
      • A chalazion is a usually painless, firm lump further back on the eyelid, caused by a blocked oil gland. It can follow a stye and may last for weeks.

      Home care:

      • Hold a clean, warm cloth against the closed eye for 5 to 10 minutes, 3 or 4 times a day, then gently massage the area.
      • Don’t squeeze or burst it, and don’t pull out the eyelashes.
      • Don’t wear eye make-up or contact lenses until it has healed, and throw away old eye make-up.
      • Keep your eyelids clean, especially if you have dandruff or blepharitis (inflamed eyelid edges), which make styes more likely.

      Antibiotics usually aren’t needed. A Cochrane review found no good evidence that they help an ordinary stye. A doctor may prescribe them if the infection is spreading.

      The old belief that a stye comes from peeking at something you shouldn’t have is just a myth. Styes are caused by bacteria, often carried from the nose to the eye on unwashed fingers.

      See a doctor if: the stye doesn’t improve after a week or two, your vision is affected, the whole eyelid or the skin around the eye becomes red and swollen, the eye itself is red and painful, or styes keep coming back. A chalazion that lasts may need a small procedure to drain it. For a red, sticky eye, see our guide to conjunctivitis.

      Where to get a boil or stye checked

      A GP can drain a boil, prescribe antibiotics if needed, check your blood sugar if boils keep coming back, and refer you to an eye doctor for a stubborn stye or chalazion.       Frequently asked questions

      What is bisul in English?

      Bisul is a boil, a painful pus-filled lump caused by a bacterial infection of a hair follicle.

      How do I get rid of a boil fast?

      Hold a warm compress on it for about 10 minutes up to 4 times a day to help it drain. Don’t squeeze it. See a doctor if it is large, very painful, on your face, or you have a fever.

      Should I squeeze a boil?

      No. Squeezing can push the infection deeper or spread it, especially on the face. A doctor can drain it safely if needed.

      Do boils need antibiotics?

      Most simple boils don’t. Large or spreading infections, carbuncles, facial boils, fever, or boils in people with diabetes or a weak immune system may need antibiotics.

      Why do I keep getting boils?

      Common reasons include carrying staph bacteria in the nose, diabetes, friction and sweat, or close contact with others who get boils. See a doctor to check your blood sugar and discuss clearing the bacteria.

      What is ketumbit in English?

      Ketumbit is a stye, a small painful infection on the edge of the eyelid.

      How long does a stye last?

      Usually about a week. Warm compresses help it drain. See a doctor if it lasts longer than a week or two or affects your vision.

      What is the difference between a stye and a chalazion?

      A stye is a painful infection at the eyelid edge. A chalazion is a usually painless lump further back on the eyelid from a blocked oil gland, which can last for weeks.
