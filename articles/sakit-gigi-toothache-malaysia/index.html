      Sakit gigi (toothache): causes, how to ease the pain until you see a dentist, and when it’s an emergency

      Sakit gigi is the Malay word for toothache. It is usually caused by tooth decay, a cracked tooth, gum disease or an infection, and it won’t go away for good until a dentist treats the cause. Until then, the right painkillers and a few simple steps can help. Facial swelling that spreads, or difficulty swallowing or breathing, is an emergency.

      Key points
      • Common causes are tooth decay, an abscess, a cracked tooth or loose filling, gum disease, an infected wisdom tooth, sensitive teeth and grinding.
      • Dental guidelines recommend an anti-inflammatory painkiller such as ibuprofen, alone or with paracetamol, as the first choice for toothache. But avoid ibuprofen if you might have dengue.
      • Don’t put aspirin on your gum: it burns the gum and gives little relief. Clove oil is unproven for toothache and dangerous if swallowed by children.
      • Antibiotics don’t cure toothache. An abscess needs the tooth treated or drained, and antibiotics are used only for spreading infection or fever.
      • Go to an emergency department for facial swelling spreading to the eye or neck, difficulty swallowing, speaking or breathing, or a high fever.

      What causes toothache?

      • Tooth decay reaching the nerve. In Malaysia, a 2020 national survey found tooth decay in 85% of people aged 15 and over.
      • A dental abscess: a pocket of pus from an infected tooth or gum, causing intense throbbing pain, a bad taste, swelling and sometimes fever
      • A cracked tooth or loose filling
      • Gum disease, which affected 38% of Malaysian adults in the 2020 survey
      • A wisdom tooth that has only partly come through and become infected (pericoronitis)
      • Sensitive teeth, with short sharp pain from hot, cold or sweet food
      • Grinding or clenching, which can cause jaw pain and worn or broken teeth
      • Sinusitis, which can cause pain that feels like toothache. See our resdung and sinus guide.

      Jaw pain together with chest pain can be a heart attack. Call 999. See our guide to chest pain.

      How to ease toothache until you see a dentist

      Painkillers: the American Dental Association’s 2024 guideline recommends an anti-inflammatory painkiller (NSAID) such as ibuprofen, alone or with paracetamol, as the first choice for toothache in adults and teenagers. Studies after wisdom tooth removal found ibuprofen combined with paracetamol worked better than opioid painkillers, with fewer side effects.

      • Ibuprofen (adults): usually 200 to 400mg up to 3 times a day with food, at least 4 hours apart. It isn’t suitable for everyone, including people with stomach ulcers, some types of asthma, heart, liver or kidney disease, or in pregnancy.
      • Paracetamol (adults): 500mg to 1g, at least 4 hours apart, no more than 4g in 24 hours. Many cold remedies also contain paracetamol.
      • Dengue: if you have a fever and could have dengue, use paracetamol only and avoid ibuprofen and aspirin.

      See our painkiller guide.

      Other things that help:

      • rinse with warm salt water (half a teaspoon of salt in a glass of water), but not for young children
      • eat soft food and chew on the other side
      • avoid very hot, very cold or sweet food and drink
      • use a soft toothbrush, and don’t smoke
      • a pharmacist can suggest a pain-relief mouth gel

      Avoid:

      • Putting aspirin on the gum. It dissolves into an acid that burns the gum and gives little or no pain relief.
      • Relying on clove oil. There is no good evidence for toothache, and swallowing it can poison young children.
      • Numbing gels with benzocaine for babies’ teething. The US FDA warns they can cause a dangerous blood condition.

      Do I need antibiotics?

      Usually not. Dental guidelines advise against antibiotics for an inflamed tooth nerve, or for an abscess that hasn’t spread, when dental treatment is available. Antibiotics don’t fix the tooth, and the pain returns.

      A dental abscess won’t go away on its own. The dentist treats it by draining it, with a root canal treatment or by removing the tooth. Antibiotics are added if the infection is spreading, or if you have a fever or feel unwell.

      Abscess and emergency signs

      See a dentist urgently for intense pain with swelling of the face or jaw, a bad taste, swollen glands in the neck, a fever or difficulty opening your mouth.

      Go to an emergency department or call 999 if you have:

      • difficulty breathing, speaking or swallowing
      • drooling, or a swollen or raised tongue
      • swelling around the eye or down the neck, or problems with your vision
      • a high fever with facial swelling

      These can be signs of a spreading infection under the tongue and in the neck (Ludwig’s angina), which can block the airway. Don’t drive yourself.

      A knocked-out adult tooth: hold it by the crown, not the root. If it is dirty, rinse it briefly in milk, salt water or saliva, then push it back into the socket and bite on a clean cloth. If you can’t, keep it in milk or in your mouth against your cheek, and see a dentist straight away. Never put a baby tooth back in.

      Toothache and teething in children

      • Teething babies: a chilled (not frozen) teething ring and gentle gum massage can help. Sugar-free paracetamol can be given from 2 months and ibuprofen from 3 months, at the dose on the pack.
      • Tooth decay in young children is very common in Malaysia. In a national survey, 71% of 5-year-olds had decay, most of it untreated.
      • Children aged 1 to 6 receive free dental treatment at government dental clinics. See our guide to government dental clinics.
      • Brush your child’s teeth twice a day with fluoride toothpaste: a smear for under-3s and a pea-sized amount for ages 3 to 6, with supervision.

      Preventing toothache

      • Brush twice a day for 2 minutes with fluoride toothpaste. Spit out, don’t rinse.
      • Clean between your teeth with floss or interdental brushes.
      • Cut down on sugary food and drinks, especially between meals.
      • Have a dental check-up regularly, even if nothing hurts.
      • Avoid smoking, betel quid and alcohol, the main risks for oral cancer. More than 60% of oral cancers in Malaysia are found late. See a dentist or doctor for any mouth sore that hasn’t healed within 2 to 3 weeks. See our guide to mouth ulcers.

      Water fluoridation helps protect teeth. About three-quarters of Malaysians had fluoridated water in 2018, but coverage is low in some states, such as Sabah.

      Find help for toothache

      For toothache, see a dentist. Our directory lists GP clinics rather than dentists, but a GP can help with pain relief, fever or facial swelling when you can’t reach a dentist, and many are open late and at weekends.

      Frequently asked questions

      What is the best ubat sakit gigi?

      Dental guidelines recommend ibuprofen, alone or with paracetamol, as the first choice for adults. Avoid ibuprofen if you might have dengue, and see a dentist to treat the cause.

      How can I stop toothache at night?

      Take an appropriate painkiller, rinse with warm salt water, and avoid very hot, cold or sweet food. Painkillers only help for a while, so book a dentist.

      Can I put aspirin on my tooth?

      No. Aspirin placed on the gum burns it and gives little or no relief. Swallow painkillers as directed instead.

      Will antibiotics cure my toothache?

      No. Antibiotics don’t fix the tooth. They are only used when an infection is spreading or you have a fever, along with dental treatment.

      When is toothache an emergency?

      When there is swelling spreading to the eye or neck, difficulty swallowing, speaking or breathing, or a high fever. Go to an emergency department.

      Does clove oil work for toothache?

      There is no good evidence that it works for toothache, and swallowing it can be dangerous for young children.

      What should I do with a knocked-out tooth?

      For an adult tooth, hold it by the crown, rinse it in milk if dirty, put it back and bite on a cloth, or keep it in milk, and see a dentist straight away. Never put a baby tooth back.
