      G6PD deficiency in Malaysia: newborn screening, jaundice, what to avoid, and living with it

      G6PD deficiency is an inherited condition in which red blood cells lack enough of an enzyme that protects them. Most of the time people with it are completely well, but certain medicines, chemicals, foods and infections can make the red cells break down suddenly. Every baby born in Malaysia is screened for it at birth.

      Key points
      • G6PD deficiency affects about 3 in 100 Malaysian newborns. It is more common in boys, and in Chinese and Malay than Indian families, but girls can be affected too.
      • All babies in Malaysia are screened using blood from the umbilical cord at birth. The result is recorded in the child’s health record book.
      • Babies with G6PD deficiency are more likely to develop severe jaundice, so they are monitored closely in the first days of life.
      • The best-proven triggers are fava beans (broad beans), naphthalene mothballs, henna, infections and a short list of medicines. Many food “avoid lists” circulating online have little evidence.
      • Tell every doctor and pharmacist that you or your child has G6PD deficiency before taking any new medicine or supplement.

      What is G6PD deficiency? (G6PD maksud)

      G6PD (glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase) is an enzyme that protects red blood cells from damage. People with G6PD deficiency have too little of it, so when they are exposed to certain triggers, their red cells can break down quickly. This is called haemolysis, and it causes anaemia and jaundice.

      • It is inherited through a gene on the X chromosome, so it is more common and usually more severe in boys. Girls who carry the gene can also be affected, sometimes as much as boys.
      • Worldwide, at least 400 million people have it. In Malaysia, studies put it at about 3 to 4% of newborns, more common in Chinese and Malay than Indian families.
      • It is lifelong, but it isn’t a disease you “catch”, and most people with it live completely normal, healthy lives.

      Newborn screening in Malaysia

      • Malaysia has screened newborns for G6PD deficiency since 1986.
      • Under the Ministry of Health programme, blood is taken from the umbilical cord right after birth and tested for G6PD deficiency, together with congenital hypothyroidism.
      • The result is recorded in your child’s health record book (Buku Rekod Kesihatan Kanak-kanak). Keep it safe and show it to doctors.
      • The standard screening test can miss partial deficiency, which is more common in girls. If a girl has unexplained jaundice or anaemia, or there is a family history, ask the doctor whether more testing is needed.

      G6PD deficiency and jaundice in babies

      Babies with G6PD deficiency are more likely to develop severe newborn jaundice. Untreated, very high levels of bilirubin (the yellow pigment) can cause brain damage (kernicterus) and hearing loss. That is why these babies are watched closely and have their bilirubin checked in the first days of life, and may stay in hospital a little longer. Jaundice is treated with light therapy (phototherapy).

      See a doctor urgently if your baby:

      • has yellow skin or eyes that are spreading or getting deeper
      • is very sleepy, floppy or feeding poorly
      • looks pale, has dark urine, or has any breathing difficulty or fits

      For babies with G6PD deficiency:

      • Don’t store the baby’s clothes, or yours, with mothballs (naphthalene, ubat gegat), and don’t use them in the home.
      • Avoid henna on the baby or on a breastfeeding mother.
      • Check with a doctor before giving the baby, or taking while breastfeeding, any herbal or traditional medicine. Some Chinese herbal medicines, such as those containing huang lian (Coptis), have been linked to severe jaundice in babies with G6PD deficiency.

      Breastfeeding mothers are generally advised to avoid the same triggers as the baby. See also our guide to fever in children, as infections can also trigger haemolysis.

      What to avoid

      A 2016 systematic review by a Malaysian-led team found that the triggers with consistent evidence are:

      • Fava beans (broad beans, sometimes called kacang parang), including foods made from fava bean flour or protein. Cooking doesn’t make them safe. Check labels.
      • Naphthalene mothballs
      • Henna
      • certain chemical dyes

      Infections are also a common trigger, so see a doctor if a person with G6PD deficiency is unwell with a fever.

      Medicines: an international pharmacogenetics guideline (CPIC) lists a small number of medicines as high risk, including dapsone, primaquine, tafenoquine, rasburicase and methylene blue, with nitrofurantoin to be used with caution. Many other medicines that appear on older lists, including some antibiotics and antimalarials, are now considered low risk at normal doses, but hospital lists in Malaysia are often more cautious. Always tell your doctor and pharmacist, and let them decide. Don’t avoid a needed medicine on your own.

      Food myths: lists circulating online often tell people with G6PD deficiency to avoid soya, bitter gourd, blueberries, menthol and many other foods. The review found no good evidence for most of these. Vitamin C at normal doses is fine. Only extremely high doses have caused problems.

      Signs of a haemolytic crisis

      After exposure to a trigger, symptoms usually develop within hours to 2 to 3 days:

      • sudden paleness, tiredness and weakness
      • dark, tea- or cola-coloured urine
      • yellow skin or eyes (jaundice)
      • a fast heartbeat or breathlessness
      • tummy or back pain

      This needs urgent medical care. Go to an emergency department, especially for a child. Most people recover fully once the trigger is removed, but some need a blood transfusion. See our guide on clinic or emergency department.

      Living with G6PD deficiency

      • Most people with G6PD deficiency are healthy and never have a crisis.
      • Tell every doctor, dentist and pharmacist before any new medicine, including over-the-counter, herbal and traditional remedies and supplements. Some children carry a G6PD alert card.
      • Avoid fava beans, mothballs and henna.
      • Tell your obstetrician in a future pregnancy, so the baby can be monitored.

      G6PD deficiency is different from thalassaemia, another inherited red blood cell condition common in Malaysia. See our guide to thalassaemia screening.

      Where to get advice

      A GP can check whether a medicine is safe with G6PD deficiency, assess jaundice or anaemia, and refer you to a paediatrician or haematologist if needed. Our directory lists GP clinics across Kuala Lumpur, Selangor and other states, including clinics open 24 hours.

      Frequently asked questions

      What does G6PD deficiency mean?

      It means the red blood cells lack enough of the G6PD enzyme, so they can break down when exposed to certain triggers such as fava beans, mothballs, some medicines or infections.

      Is G6PD deficiency serious?

      Most people live normal, healthy lives. The main risks are severe jaundice in newborns and sudden anaemia after a trigger, both of which can be prevented or treated.

      How is G6PD deficiency tested in Malaysia?

      All newborns are screened using blood from the umbilical cord at birth, and the result is recorded in the child’s health record book.

      What foods should someone with G6PD deficiency avoid?

      Mainly fava beans (broad beans) and foods made from them. Many other foods on online lists have little evidence behind them.

      Which medicines should be avoided with G6PD deficiency?

      High-risk medicines include dapsone, primaquine, tafenoquine, rasburicase and methylene blue. Always tell your doctor and pharmacist, who can check any medicine.

      Are mothballs dangerous for babies with G6PD deficiency?

      Yes. Naphthalene mothballs can trigger red cell breakdown. Don’t use them at home or store baby clothes with them.

      Can girls have G6PD deficiency?

      Yes. It is more common in boys, but girls can be affected, and the standard newborn screening test can miss partial deficiency in girls.

      What are the signs of a G6PD crisis?

      Sudden paleness, tiredness, dark tea-coloured urine, yellow skin or eyes, and a fast heartbeat or breathlessness. Seek urgent care.
