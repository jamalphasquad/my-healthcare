      Normal body temperature (suhu badan normal) and fever in adults: the numbers and when to see a doctor

      For most adults, normal body temperature is about 36.5°C to 37.5°C, and a reading of 38°C or above by mouth is generally called a fever. The old figure of 37°C is only a rough average. Your temperature changes with the time of day, your age and how you take it. Here is how to read the numbers, and when a fever needs a doctor.

      Key points
      • Modern studies put average adult temperature nearer 36.6°C than 37°C. It is lowest in the early morning and can reach 37.7°C by late afternoon.
      • A temperature of 38°C or above by mouth is generally a fever. An ear reading is about 0.6°C higher than by mouth, and a forehead reading is about 0.6°C lower.
      • Older people may have a serious infection with little or no fever. New confusion or sudden weakness can be the first sign.
      • In Malaysia, use paracetamol for fever and avoid aspirin, mefenamic acid and ibuprofen until dengue has been ruled out.
      • See a doctor if a fever lasts more than 2 days without improving, and urgently for confusion, a stiff neck, purple spots, breathlessness or dengue warning signs. In dengue, you can get worse as the fever falls.

      What is a normal body temperature?

      The familiar figure of 37°C comes from a German doctor’s measurements in 1851, which were taken in the armpit. Modern studies have found lower averages:

      • A 1992 US study of 148 healthy adults found an average oral temperature of 36.8°C, with an upper limit of 37.7°C.
      • A 2017 study of more than 35,000 UK patients found an average of 36.6°C.
      • A 2023 US study of nearly 400,000 outpatient visits found that no group had an average as high as 37°C.

      “Normal” differs from person to person, and it changes with:

      • Time of day: lowest in the early morning and highest in the late afternoon
      • Age: tends to be lower in older people
      • Menstrual cycle: about 0.3 to 0.7°C higher in the second half of the cycle
      • Your thermometer and where you take the reading

      Small Malaysian and Japanese comparisons suggest that people living in hot climates may have slightly different resting temperatures, but nothing supports using a different fever threshold in Malaysia.

      What temperature is a fever?

      Oral readingWhat it generally means
      Below 35°CHypothermia. See a doctor right away
      About 36.5 to 37.5°CNormal for most adults
      37.5 to 37.9°CLow-grade fever (there is no agreed range)
      38°C and aboveFever
      Above 39.4°CCall or see a doctor
      Above 40°CSee a doctor right away
      Above 41.5°CHyperpyrexia, a medical emergency

      The site of the reading matters. As a rough guide, ear and rectal readings are about 0.6°C higher than oral readings, and forehead readings are about 0.6°C lower. That means a forehead reading of about 37.4°C can correspond to an oral reading of 38°C. Armpit readings are the least reliable.

      You can feel hot, shivery or unwell with a reading below 38°C. How you feel matters as well as the number.

      Older people: more than half of older long-term care residents with a serious infection don’t develop a typical fever. For them, a temperature above 37.8°C, repeated readings above 37.2°C, or a rise of more than 1.1°C above their usual temperature can be significant. A low temperature can also be a sign of sepsis.

      How to take your temperature properly

      • Use a digital thermometer under the tongue, as the NHS advises. Keep your mouth closed until it beeps.
      • Wait after hot or cold drinks. A small study suggested about 15 minutes after a cold drink and 23 minutes after a hot one. Rest after exercise too.
      • Forehead (infrared) thermometers: follow the instructions, make sure your forehead is dry and uncovered, and don’t use one in sunlight or a draught. Let it adjust to the room for 10 to 30 minutes if it has been moved. A 2015 review found that home-style thermometers, including ear, forehead, armpit and oral types, missed around a third of fevers, and forehead devices aren’t recommended for detecting one. If you feel ill, go by how you feel as well as the number.
      • Avoid mercury thermometers. The WHO has called for them to be phased out, because of the danger if they break.

      Treating a fever at home

      A fever is part of the body’s response to infection, and it doesn’t always need treating. Medicine is usually unnecessary below about 38.3°C if you feel all right. Take it if you feel unwell.

      • Rest and drink plenty of fluids. In suspected dengue, the Malaysian guideline advises more than 2 litres a day, and not plain water alone.
      • Paracetamol: 500mg to 1g, at least 4 hours apart, with no more than 4g in 24 hours. If you weigh under 50kg, you may need less. Many cold and flu remedies also contain paracetamol, so check the label. See our guide to painkillers.
      • Avoid aspirin, mefenamic acid and ibuprofen until dengue has been ruled out, because of the risk of bleeding. Guidance from other countries may recommend ibuprofen for fever, but this advice doesn’t account for dengue.
      • Cooling: Malaysian dengue guidance lists tepid sponging. Other guidance says extra cooling is only needed for very high temperatures, around 41°C. Don’t use rubbing alcohol.
      • Antibiotics don’t treat viral fevers, including dengue, flu and most coughs and colds.
      • Don’t have injections, massage or cupping when you have suspected dengue.

      Common causes of fever in Malaysia

      • Dengue: fever for 2 to 7 days, with headache, body aches and sometimes a rash
      • Influenza, colds and COVID-19
      • Urinary tract infections, especially with back pain or burning urine
      • Leptospirosis, from water or soil contaminated by animal urine, which can cause fever, red eyes and jaundice
      • Typhoid: prolonged high fever and tummy pain
      • Malaria, after time in forest areas of Sabah, Sarawak, Kelantan or Pahang, or travel abroad
      • Tuberculosis: a long-lasting cough, night sweats and weight loss

      A fever without a clear cause: doctors use the term “fever of unknown origin” for a temperature above 38.3°C lasting more than 3 weeks, with no cause found after investigation. Infections, cancers and autoimmune conditions can all cause it.

      Fever or heat illness? In hot weather, heat exhaustion causes heavy sweating, cool clammy skin and tiredness, and should improve within 30 minutes of cooling down and drinking. Heatstroke is an emergency: look for confusion, seizures or collapse, and call 999. Malaysia recorded 45 heat illness cases, including 2 deaths, in early 2024.

      When to see a doctor

      Go to an emergency department or call 999 for a fever with:

      • confusion, unusual drowsiness or difficulty waking
      • severe headache with a stiff neck
      • a purple or dark rash that doesn’t fade
      • breathlessness or chest pain
      • a seizure
      • severe tummy pain

      Dengue warning signs need hospital care straight away, even if the fever is going down:

      • tummy pain or tenderness
      • vomiting or diarrhoea more than 3 times in 24 hours
      • bleeding from the nose or gums, black stools or unusually heavy periods
      • drowsiness, restlessness or irritability
      • cold, clammy skin or difficulty breathing

      See a doctor promptly if:

      • your fever has lasted 24 to 48 hours without improving, and in any case by 3 to 4 days
      • your temperature is above 39.4°C
      • you are pregnant, over 65, or have a weakened immune system or a long-term condition such as diabetes
      • you have recently had a baby or a miscarriage
      • you have recently been in a forest area or abroad

      Frequently asked questions

      What is a normal body temperature for an adult?

      About 36.5°C to 37.5°C for most adults, although modern studies put the average nearer 36.6°C. It changes through the day.

      Is 37.5°C a fever?

      It is usually considered at most a low-grade fever when taken by mouth. A fever is generally 38°C or above. A reading of 37.5°C on the forehead may be closer to 38°C by mouth.

      Is 38°C a high fever?

      It is a fever, but there is no agreed definition of a high fever. See a doctor if it lasts more than 2 days, if it goes above 39.4°C, or if you have any danger signs.

      Is 36°C normal?

      It can be normal, especially early in the morning or in older people. A reading below 35°C is hypothermia. A low temperature with feeling very unwell may be a sign of serious infection.

      Which thermometer is the most accurate at home?

      A digital thermometer used under the tongue is a good choice. Forehead and ear thermometers are convenient, but they can miss fevers.

      Can I take ibuprofen for a fever in Malaysia?

      Avoid ibuprofen, aspirin and mefenamic acid until dengue has been ruled out, because they can increase the risk of bleeding. Use paracetamol instead.

      How many days of fever is too long?

      See a doctor if a fever lasts more than 24 to 48 hours without improving, and in any case by 3 to 4 days. In dengue season, see a doctor early.
