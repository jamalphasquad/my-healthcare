      Constipation (sembelit): causes, what helps, laxatives, and warning signs

      Constipation (sembelit) means passing stools less often than usual, or having hard stools that are difficult to pass. It is very common, especially in women, older people and during pregnancy, and it often improves with simple changes. But a new change in your bowel habit, particularly after 50 or with bleeding, should be checked.

      Key points
      • Constipation usually means fewer than 3 bowel movements a week, or hard stools, straining or a feeling of not emptying. About 1 in 10 Malaysian adults in primary care are affected.
      • Malaysia recommends 20 to 30 g of fibre a day, but most adults fall short. Add fibre gradually with enough water.
      • Kiwifruit, psyllium and prunes have good evidence. Regular exercise and a set toilet time help too.
      • Macrogol (PEG) and psyllium are well-supported laxatives. Stimulant laxatives such as senna and bisacodyl work within 6 to 12 hours and are for short-term use. Check with a doctor if you need one for more than a week.
      • See a doctor for blood in your stool, weight loss, anaemia, a new change in bowel habit after 50, or a family history of bowel cancer. Severe tummy pain with no wind or stool is an emergency.

      What is constipation?

      Everyone’s bowel habit is different. Doctors usually call it constipation if, for several weeks, you have:

      • fewer than 3 bowel movements a week
      • hard or lumpy stools
      • straining, or a feeling of not emptying fully or of something blocking the way

      Worldwide, about 9 to 20% of people are affected, and it is about twice as common in women. Malaysian studies have found it in about 11% of adults attending primary care, and 15% of older clinic patients.

      Common causes

      • not eating enough fibre or drinking enough fluids
      • not moving enough, or long periods of sitting or bed rest
      • ignoring the urge to go, or a change in routine, such as travel or shift work
      • stress, and irritable bowel syndrome
      • pregnancy and older age
      • Medicines, including iron tablets, opioid painkillers such as codeine, some antacids, some antidepressants and calcium channel blockers
      • conditions such as diabetes, an underactive thyroid and Parkinson’s disease

      Straining can cause piles (buasir) and small tears around the anus.

      What helps (cara atasi sembelit)

      • Fibre: Malaysia’s recommended intake is 20 to 30 g a day, but most adults eat less, and Malaysians average under 2 servings each of fruit and vegetables a day. Increase it gradually over a few weeks to avoid bloating.
      • Foods with the best evidence: a 2025 British Dietetic Association guideline found good support for kiwifruit (2 to 3 a day for at least 4 weeks), psyllium fibre, and rye bread. Prunes are also useful. It found less support for a general “high-fibre diet” for long-term constipation, so the type of fibre matters.
      • Fluids: drink about 1.5 to 2 litres a day.
      • Move more: aim for 150 minutes of activity a week.
      • Toilet routine: go when you feel the urge, try after breakfast, and don’t rush. Put your feet on a low stool so your knees are above your hips.

      • See a doctor if pharmacy laxatives haven’t helped after 3 days, and don’t use them for more than 7 days without advice.
      • At recommended doses, stimulant laxatives are unlikely to damage the bowel, but overuse can cause dehydration and low potassium.

      Be careful with “detox” teas and slimming products. In 2024 and 2025, Singapore’s food authority found the undeclared laxative sennosides in slimming products made in Malaysia, and the Ministry of Health has warned about unregistered slimming products with hidden drugs. These can cause dehydration and low potassium.

      Babies and children (sembelit bayi dan kanak-kanak)

      • Babies: breastfed babies may go after every feed or only once a week, and both can be normal if the stool is soft. Bottle-fed babies usually go every 1 to 3 days. If you think a baby under 1 is constipated, see a doctor. Don’t give laxatives or enemas without medical advice.
      • Children: signs are fewer than 3 stools a week, hard pellets, pain, or soiling their underwear. Pain makes them hold back, which makes it worse. Encourage them to sit on the toilet after meals with their feet supported, and give fibre and fluids.
      • Children often need macrogol from a doctor, sometimes for several months. See a GP early.

      See a doctor urgently if a newborn didn’t pass stool in the first 48 hours, if constipation began in the first weeks of life, or if there is a swollen tummy with vomiting, or leg weakness.

      Constipation in pregnancy

      Try fibre, fluids and activity first. If needed, bulk-forming laxatives, then lactulose or macrogol, are preferred, as they aren’t absorbed. Senna is used only if those don’t work. Iron tablets can make constipation worse, so ask if a different preparation may help. See our pregnancy guide.

      When to see a doctor

      See a doctor if you have constipation with:

      • blood in your stool, or dark stools
      • unexplained weight loss or anaemia
      • a new, lasting change in your bowel habit, especially from age 50
      • a family history of bowel cancer
      • a lump in your tummy, or constipation that doesn’t improve after several weeks

      The Ministry of Health’s bowel cancer guideline advises referral for a colonoscopy when such symptoms don’t resolve in 4 to 6 weeks. See our guide to bowel cancer screening.

      Go to an emergency department for severe tummy pain with a swollen tummy, vomiting, and no wind or stool passing, which can mean a blocked bowel. Don’t eat or drink. See our guide to stomach pain.

      Older people can develop faecal impaction, where a hard mass of stool blocks the bowel. It can cause confusion, difficulty passing urine, poor appetite, and watery leakage that looks like diarrhoea. Don’t give anti-diarrhoea medicine. See a doctor.

      Where to get help

      A GP can look for causes, review your medicines, recommend a suitable laxative, and refer you for a colonoscopy if needed. Our directory lists GP clinics across Kuala Lumpur, Selangor and other states.

      Frequently asked questions

      What is sembelit in English?

      Sembelit is constipation: passing stools less often than usual, or hard stools that are difficult to pass.

      How often should I go to the toilet?

      It varies from person to person. Fewer than 3 times a week, with hard stools or straining, usually counts as constipation.

      What foods help constipation?

      Kiwifruit, prunes, psyllium fibre, and fruit, vegetables and wholegrains generally. Increase fibre gradually and drink enough water.

      What is the best laxative?

      Psyllium and macrogol have good evidence for regular use. Stimulant laxatives such as senna or bisacodyl work faster and are mainly for short-term use.

      Are laxatives safe?

      Yes, at the recommended dose for short periods. Long-term or heavy use should be supervised by a doctor. Don’t use them for weight loss.

      How do I help my constipated child?

      Give fibre and fluids, and have them sit on the toilet after meals with their feet supported. See a GP early, as children often need a laxative for some months.

      Is constipation in pregnancy normal?

      Yes, it is common. Try fibre and fluids first, then ask about bulk-forming laxatives, lactulose or macrogol.

      When is constipation serious?

      With blood in the stool, weight loss, anaemia, a new change in bowel habit after 50, or severe pain with vomiting and no wind.
