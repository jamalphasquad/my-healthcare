      Fever in children (demam budak): normal temperature, fever medicine, febrile seizures and when to see a doctor

      A fever is the body’s normal response to infection, and most fevers in children are caused by viruses that clear up within a few days. How your child looks and behaves matters more than the number on the thermometer. In Malaysia, a fever that lasts more than 2 to 3 days also needs checking for dengue.

      Key points
      • A temperature of 38°C or higher is a fever. Most fevers last 1 to 4 days.
      • Any baby under 3 months with a temperature of 38°C or more, or 3 to 6 months with 39°C or more, needs to see a doctor urgently.
      • Give paracetamol or ibuprofen if your child is distressed, not just to bring the number down. If dengue is possible, give paracetamol only, not ibuprofen.
      • See a doctor if a fever lasts more than 2 to 3 days, as a blood test may be needed to check for dengue. Dengue warning signs often appear as the fever goes down.
      • Febrile seizures (sawan demam) affect 3 to 4 in 100 young children. They are frightening but don’t cause brain damage. Lay the child on their side and call 999 if it lasts more than 5 minutes.

      What is a normal temperature? (Suhu badan normal)

      TemperatureWhat it means
      Up to about 37.4°CNormal
      37.5–37.9°CSlightly raised (low-grade)
      38°C or higherFever

      How to measure:

      • Use a digital thermometer. Feeling the forehead with your hand isn’t reliable. Don’t use mercury thermometers.
      • For young children, put the thermometer in the armpit, with the arm held against the body, until it beeps. Armpit readings are slightly lower than true body temperature.
      • Ear thermometers can be used from 6 months, and under-the-tongue readings from about 4 years.
      • Forehead (infrared) thermometers are convenient but can give false readings, for example if the child has been in the sun or a hot room.
      • Wait a few minutes after a bath or after taking off a blanket before measuring.

      Caring for a child with a fever

      • Give plenty of fluids, little and often: water, milk, or oral rehydration salts. Keep breastfeeding.
      • Dress your child in light clothing and use a light sheet. Don’t over-wrap them or bundle them in blankets.
      • Don’t sponge your child with cold or ice water, or give cold baths. It makes them uncomfortable and doesn’t help.
      • Let them rest, and offer food if they want it. There is no need to “starve a fever”.
      • Check on them regularly, including during the night.
      • Keep them home from school or nursery until they have had no fever for 24 hours without fever medicine.

      Fever medicine (ubat demam budak)

      Fever itself isn’t harmful. Medicine is to make a distressed child more comfortable. If your child is playing and drinking, they may not need it.

      ParacetamolIbuprofen
      Usual dose15 mg per kg of body weight5 to 10 mg per kg of body weight
      How oftenEvery 4 to 6 hours, no more than 4 doses in 24 hoursEvery 6 to 8 hours, with food or milk
      AgeFrom 2 months. Ask a doctor first for younger babiesFrom 3 months and over 5 kg
      Avoid if—Possible dengue, chickenpox, dehydration, or asthma (unless a doctor advises)
      • Dose by your child’s weight, not just age, and use the measuring syringe or cup that comes with the medicine. Check the strength on the label, as children’s syrups come in different strengths.
      • In Malaysia, dengue is a real possibility with any fever. Ibuprofen and aspirin can raise the risk of bleeding in dengue, so use paracetamol unless a doctor has ruled dengue out.
      • Don’t routinely alternate paracetamol and ibuprofen unless a doctor advises it.
      • Never give aspirin to children under 16.

      When to see a doctor

      Call 999 or go to an emergency department if your child:

      • has a rash that doesn’t fade when you press a glass against it
      • has a stiff neck, or is bothered by bright light
      • is floppy, very drowsy or hard to wake
      • is struggling to breathe, breathing very fast, or the chest sucks in with each breath
      • has blue, grey, pale or blotchy skin or lips, or cold hands and feet
      • has a fit (seizure) for the first time

      See a doctor the same day if:

      • your baby is under 3 months with 38°C or more, or 3 to 6 months with 39°C or more
      • your child shows signs of dehydration: fewer wet nappies, dry mouth, sunken eyes, no tears
      • they can’t keep fluids down, or aren’t feeding
      • the fever has lasted more than 2 to 3 days, or 5 days or more at any time
      • you are worried, or your child seems to be getting worse

      Fever and dengue

      Dengue usually causes a high fever for 3 to 5 days. The dangerous stage often starts as the fever goes down, when a falling temperature can give false reassurance. Watch for:

      • tummy pain, or vomiting 3 or more times a day
      • bleeding from the gums or nose, or blood in vomit or stools
      • being restless, very tired or drowsy
      • cold, clammy skin, or passing little urine

      Any of these needs a hospital straight away. If your child has had a fever for 2 to 3 days, see a doctor, who may arrange a blood test. See our guide to dengue in the first three days.

      Febrile seizures (sawan demam)

      A febrile seizure is a fit triggered by a fever. It affects 3 to 4 in 100 children, usually between 6 months and 5 years old. The child may stiffen, twitch or jerk, roll their eyes and become unresponsive, usually for less than 5 minutes.

      What to do:

      1. Stay calm and note the time.
      2. Lay your child on their side on a safe surface, away from anything hard or sharp.
      3. Don’t put anything in their mouth, and don’t try to hold them down.
      4. Don’t try to cool them by sponging or undressing them during the seizure.

      Call 999 if it is your child’s first seizure, it lasts more than 5 minutes, it affects only one side of the body, there is more than one seizure in 24 hours, or your child is still very drowsy an hour later.

      The good news: febrile seizures don’t cause brain damage, and very few children go on to have epilepsy. About 3 in 10 have another one with a later fever. Fever medicines don’t prevent febrile seizures, so there is no need to wake your child to give doses.

      Common causes of fever in Malaysian children

      • colds, flu and other viral infections. See our guides to influenza and RSV.
      • hand, foot and mouth disease and chickenpox
      • tonsillitis and ear infections
      • dengue
      • urinary tract infections, which in babies may cause only fever and irritability
      • vaccinations: a mild fever within a day or two of a vaccine is common and usually settles within 1 to 2 days. A fever 1 to 2 weeks after the MMR or chickenpox vaccine can also happen. See our vaccination schedule guide.

      Frequently asked questions

      What temperature is a fever in a child?

      38°C or higher. Up to about 37.4°C is normal, and 37.5 to 37.9°C is slightly raised.

      When should I take my child to the doctor for a fever?

      Straight away for a baby under 3 months with 38°C or more, or any danger signs such as a rash that doesn’t fade, breathing difficulty or drowsiness. Otherwise, if the fever lasts more than 2 to 3 days, or your child is dehydrated or getting worse.

      How much paracetamol can I give my child?

      Usually 15 mg per kg of body weight every 4 to 6 hours, with no more than 4 doses in 24 hours. Check the strength on the label and use the measuring device provided.

      Can I give ibuprofen for fever in Malaysia?

      Only from 3 months, and not if dengue is possible, as it can raise the risk of bleeding. Use paracetamol unless a doctor has ruled out dengue.

      Should I sponge my child with cold water?

      No. Cold sponging or ice baths make children uncomfortable and don’t help. Dress them lightly and give plenty of fluids.

      What should I do if my child has a febrile seizure?

      Note the time, lay them on their side somewhere safe, don’t put anything in their mouth, and call 999 if it is the first one or lasts more than 5 minutes.

      Do febrile seizures cause brain damage?

      No. They are frightening, but they don’t cause brain damage, and very few children go on to have epilepsy.

      When can my child go back to school after a fever?

      When they have had no fever for 24 hours without fever medicine and are feeling better.
