      Sakit tumit dan tapak kaki (heel and sole pain): plantar fasciitis, stretches and when it’s a sign of something else

      Sakit tumit means heel pain, and sakit tapak kaki means pain in the sole of the foot. The most common cause in adults is plantar fasciitis, where the thick band of tissue under the foot becomes strained. It is painful, but about 8 in 10 people recover within a year without surgery. Burning or numb soles are different, and can be a sign of nerve damage from diabetes.

      Key points
      • Plantar fasciitis causes a stabbing pain on the inner heel, worst with the first steps in the morning or after resting. It affects about 1 in 10 people at some point.
      • It is most common between ages 40 and 60, and in people who are overweight, stand for long hours, have tight calves or wear shoes with poor cushioning.
      • Stretching the calf and the sole, cushioned shoes with arch support, and ice help most. Off-the-shelf insoles work as well as custom-made ones.
      • Heel spurs are common in people with no pain, and they don’t cause plantar fasciitis. Steroid injections give short-term relief and carry a risk of tearing the fascia.
      • Burning, tingling or numb soles can mean nerve damage from diabetes or low vitamin B12. People with diabetes should have their feet checked at least once a year.

      Plantar fasciitis: the most common cause of heel pain

      The plantar fascia is a thick band of tissue running from the heel to the toes, supporting the arch of the foot. When it is overloaded, it becomes painful. Doctors now sometimes call it plantar fasciopathy, because the tissue is worn rather than truly inflamed.

      Typical signs:

      • a stabbing pain on the inner part of the heel
      • worst with the first steps in the morning, or after sitting for a while
      • eases after a few minutes of walking, then returns by the end of the day or after activity
      • hurts to climb stairs or raise your toes

      Who gets it: it is most common between the ages of 40 and 60, and in people who:

      • are overweight. See our BMI calculator.
      • stand or walk for long hours at work, especially on hard floors
      • run or dance, or have suddenly increased their activity
      • have tight calf muscles, flat feet or high arches
      • wear shoes with poor cushioning, or walk barefoot on hard surfaces

      About 80% of people improve within 12 months without surgery.

      What helps plantar fasciitis

      • Stretch: stretching the calf and the sole of the foot is one of the most effective treatments. A common routine is to hold each stretch for about 10 seconds and repeat it several times a day, especially before your first steps in the morning. For the sole, sit down, cross your foot over your knee and gently pull your toes back towards your shin.
      • Strengthen: exercises such as slow heel raises worked better than stretching alone in one trial at 3 months.
      • Footwear: wear cushioned shoes with good arch support, at home as well as outside. Off-the-shelf insoles or heel cups worked as well as custom-made ones. Avoid flip-flops, backless slippers, high heels and going barefoot on hard floors.
      • Ice: roll your foot over a frozen water bottle for up to 20 minutes, a few times a day.
      • Rest from painful activity: swap running for cycling or swimming for a while.
      • Lose weight if you are overweight.
      • Painkillers: paracetamol, or ibuprofen tablets or gel, for short periods. Don’t take anti-inflammatory painkillers for more than a month without seeing a doctor, and avoid them if you might have dengue. See our painkiller guide.
      • Physiotherapy can help, including taping, which gives short-term relief. Evidence for night splints is mixed.

      If it isn’t getting better:

      • Steroid injections give short-term relief at best. Repeated injections carry a risk of the fascia tearing and thinning of the heel’s fat pad.
      • Shockwave therapy is an option for long-standing cases, with mixed results.
      • Surgery is rarely needed, and is usually only considered after 12 months of other treatment.
      • Scans are usually only needed if pain lasts more than 3 months despite treatment.

      What about heel spurs?

      A heel spur is a small bony growth on the heel bone that shows up on X-rays. Spurs are common in people who have no heel pain at all, and they stay after the pain has gone. Orthopaedic surgeons say the spur doesn’t cause plantar fasciitis pain, so treatment focuses on the fascia, not the spur.

      Other causes of heel and sole pain

      CauseWhat it feels like
      Achilles tendinopathyPain and stiffness at the back of the heel, above the heel bone
      Heel fat pad bruisingDeep bruised pain in the middle of the heel, often after walking on hard ground
      Stress fracturePain that builds with activity and hurts when you squeeze the heel, often after a sudden increase in walking or running
      Tarsal tunnel syndromeBurning, tingling or numbness in the sole, from a trapped nerve
      Morton’s neuromaSharp, burning pain near the toes, like standing on a stone
      MetatarsalgiaPain in the ball of the foot
      Sever’s diseaseHeel pain in active children, at the growth plate

      Arthritis can also cause foot pain. Pain at night or deep bone pain should be checked by a doctor.

      Sakit tapak kaki as a sign of disease: diabetes and nerve damage

      Pain in the sole that feels like burning, pins and needles or numbness, especially in both feet, is different from plantar fasciitis. It can be a sign of nerve damage (peripheral neuropathy).

      • Diabetes is the most common cause. In Malaysia, about 1 in 6 adults has diabetes, and about 2 in 5 of them don’t know it.
      • Low vitamin B12 is another cause, more likely with a vegan diet, long-term metformin or acid-reducing medicines (PPIs).

      Up to half of people with diabetic nerve damage have no symptoms, so the feet can be injured without pain. Malaysia’s diabetic foot guideline recommends that everyone with diabetes has their feet checked for nerve damage and poor circulation when diagnosed and at least once a year.

      If you have diabetes, get help within 24 hours for: a wound or ulcer, signs of infection, black or dark skin, or a foot that is unexpectedly hot, red or swollen. Wear footwear at all times, indoors and outdoors, if your doctor says your feet are at risk.

      When to see a doctor

      Get urgent help if:

      • you have severe pain after an injury, or heard a snap or pop
      • you can’t walk or put weight on the foot
      • your foot looks misshapen
      • your foot is hot and swollen, or you feel feverish or shivery
      • you have diabetes and a wound, infection or colour change in your foot

      See a doctor if:

      • heel pain hasn’t improved after 2 weeks of self-care
      • you have tingling, burning or loss of feeling in your feet
      • you have diabetes and any foot pain
      • you have pain at night or deep bone pain

      Frequently asked questions

      What is sakit tumit in English?

      Heel pain. The most common cause in adults is plantar fasciitis.

      Why does my heel hurt in the morning?

      Pain with your first steps in the morning that eases as you walk is typical of plantar fasciitis, a strain of the band of tissue under the foot.

      How long does plantar fasciitis last?

      It often takes months. About 8 in 10 people improve within 12 months without surgery, and most get better with stretches and good footwear.

      What is the best treatment for plantar fasciitis?

      Calf and sole stretches, cushioned shoes with arch support or insoles, ice, and reducing painful activity. A doctor or physiotherapist can help if it doesn’t improve.

      Is a heel spur the cause of my pain?

      Usually not. Heel spurs are common in people without pain and don’t cause plantar fasciitis.

      Can burning soles be a sign of diabetes?

      Yes. Burning, tingling or numb feet can be a sign of nerve damage from diabetes or low vitamin B12. See a doctor for a check.

      Should I walk barefoot at home?

      If you have heel pain, try not to walk barefoot on hard floors. Wear supportive footwear or cushioned slippers.
