      Skin tags, keloids, lipomas and moles (tahi lalat): what they are, removal options, and which lumps need checking

      Skin tags, keloid scars, lipomas and moles are very common and usually harmless. Many people want them removed for comfort or appearance, which a doctor can do safely. But a mole that changes, or a lump that grows, should always be checked, as skin cancer in Asian skin is often found late and in unexpected places, such as the soles of the feet.

      Key points
      • Skin tags are small, soft flaps of skin in folds such as the neck and armpits. They are harmless, and are linked to weight, type 2 diabetes and pregnancy.
      • Keloids are raised scars that grow beyond the original wound. They are more common in Asian skin, and often come back after removal, so treatment usually combines steroid injections and other methods.
      • Lipomas are soft, movable fatty lumps. See a doctor for any lump that is larger than about 5 cm, hard, fixed, painful or growing.
      • Check moles using ABCDE: asymmetry, irregular border, several colours, diameter over 6 mm, and evolving (changing). In Asians, melanoma often appears on the soles, palms or under the nails.
      • In Malaysia, aesthetic procedures must be done by registered, credentialed doctors at licensed clinics, not at beauty salons. Suspicious moles should be cut out and sent to a lab, not lasered.

      Skin tags

      Skin tags are small, soft, skin-coloured or slightly darker growths, from 2 mm to a few centimetres, that hang from the skin on a thin stalk.

      • They form where skin rubs: the neck, armpits, eyelids, under the breasts, groin and around the bottom.
      • They are more common with excess weight, type 2 diabetes or insulin resistance, and in pregnancy. Lots of skin tags can be a reason to check your blood sugar: see our guide to normal blood sugar levels.
      • They are harmless and don’t need removing, but they can catch on clothing or jewellery.

      Removal: a doctor can snip them off, freeze them or remove them with heat (cautery), usually in minutes under local anaesthetic. They don’t usually come back, though new ones may form. Removal can leave a small scar or a darker mark, especially on brown skin.

      Don’t cut or tie off skin tags yourself, as they can bleed and become infected. Other growths, such as warts and seborrhoeic keratoses, can look similar. See our guide to warts. A soft lump around the anus may be a skin tag left after piles: see our guide to piles (buasir).

      Keloids (parut keloid)

      A keloid is a raised, firm, often shiny scar that keeps growing beyond the edges of the original wound. A hypertrophic scar looks similar but stays within the wound and often flattens with time.

      • Keloids are more common in darker and Asian skin, usually appear between puberty and 30, and can run in families. DermNet notes they are more common in people of Chinese origin than in those of Malay or Indian origin.
      • Common sites are the chest, shoulders, upper back, neck and earlobes.
      • They can follow ear piercings, acne, chickenpox, burns, vaccinations or surgery. See our guide to acne.
      • They can be itchy or tender, but aren’t dangerous.

      Treatment aims to flatten and soften the scar. No single treatment works best:

      • Steroid injections into the keloid are the most common, often every 4 to 6 weeks for several months. Up to half come back afterwards.
      • Silicone gel or sheets and pressure dressings, used daily for months.
      • Freezing, laser or other injections.
      • Surgery alone often makes the keloid come back bigger, so it is combined with injections or radiotherapy afterwards.

      If you are prone to keloids, avoid tattoos, piercings and cosmetic procedures you don’t need, treat acne early, and tell any doctor before surgery.

      Lipomas

      A lipoma is a soft, rubbery, slow-growing lump of fat under the skin that moves slightly when pressed. They are common on the shoulders, back, chest, arms and thighs, and are usually painless and harmless. They are more common in people with high cholesterol, type 2 diabetes or obesity.

      Removal is optional. A doctor can cut it out under local anaesthetic, and it may be sent to a lab to confirm what it is. Liposuction is an option for some, but they come back more often.

      See a doctor promptly for a lump that:

      • is larger than about 5 cm (a golf ball)
      • is growing
      • is hard, fixed, or deep under the muscle
      • is painful, red or hot
      • has come back after removal

      These can occasionally be signs of a rare cancer called a soft tissue sarcoma, and need a scan. Other common lumps include skin cysts, which shouldn’t be squeezed, and boils.

      Moles (tahi lalat) and the warning signs of melanoma

      Most moles are harmless. They are often darker on brown skin, new ones appear in childhood and the teens, and they can darken slightly in pregnancy.

      Use ABCDE to check your moles:

      SignWhat to look for
      AsymmetryThe two halves look different
      BorderRagged, notched or blurred edges
      ColourTwo or more colours, or uneven shading
      DiameterLarger than about 6 mm (a pencil eraser)
      EvolvingChanging in size, shape or colour

      Also see a doctor for a mole that looks different from your others, or that itches, bleeds, crusts or becomes sore.

      In Asian skin, look at your feet, hands and nails. Melanoma on the soles, palms and under the nails (acral melanoma) makes up a larger share of melanomas in Asian people. At Sarawak General Hospital, 62% of melanomas from 2003 to 2009 were acral, mostly on the heel and sole, and most were found at an advanced stage. Check for:

      • a new or changing dark patch on the sole or palm
      • a dark streak in a nail that is wider than 3 mm, uneven or growing, or where the dark colour spreads onto the skin around the nail, not caused by an injury

      Other skin cancers, basal cell carcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma, often appear as a pearly or scaly lump or a sore that doesn’t heal, usually on the face. In a recent Sarawak study, these were more common than melanoma.

      Mole removal: safely, by a doctor

      • Suspicious moles are cut out whole and sent to a laboratory to check for cancer. Lasering or burning away a mole destroys it without testing, which could miss a melanoma.
      • Harmless raised moles can be shaved off for cosmetic reasons, which may leave a small mark.
      • Avoid creams or online products that claim to remove moles or skin tags. They can burn the skin and scar, and they leave nothing to test.

      Choose a registered doctor, not a beauty salon. Under Malaysian Medical Council and Ministry of Health guidelines, aesthetic medical procedures must be carried out by registered doctors who hold a Letter of Credentialing and Privileging (LCP) for the procedure, at licensed clinics. The Ministry of Health has warned against aesthetic treatments, including lasers, at beauty salons, spas and other unregistered premises, which risk complications and disability. Ask to see the doctor’s practising certificate and credentials.

      Where to get a lump or mole checked

      A GP can examine a lump or mole, remove many skin tags and small lumps, and refer you to a dermatologist or surgeon if needed. Our directory lists GP clinics and aesthetic clinics across Kuala Lumpur, Selangor and other states.

      Frequently asked questions

      What causes skin tags?

      Friction where skin rubs together. They are more common with excess weight, type 2 diabetes and in pregnancy, and are harmless.

      Can I remove a skin tag myself?

      It isn’t recommended, as it can bleed and get infected. A doctor can remove it quickly by snipping, freezing or cautery.

      Why do keloids keep coming back?

      Keloids grow from an overactive healing response. Surgery alone often triggers a bigger keloid, so it is combined with steroid injections or other treatments, and up to half come back.

      Is a lipoma dangerous?

      Lipomas are almost always harmless. See a doctor for a lump that is larger than about 5 cm, hard, fixed, painful or growing.

      How do I know if a mole is cancerous?

      Use ABCDE: asymmetry, irregular border, several colours, diameter over 6 mm, and evolving. See a doctor for any mole that changes, itches or bleeds.

      Can melanoma appear on the feet?

      Yes. In Asian people, melanoma is relatively often found on the soles, palms or under the nails, and is often diagnosed late.

      Can I have a mole removed at a beauty salon?

      No. In Malaysia, aesthetic procedures must be done by credentialed doctors at licensed clinics. Suspicious moles should be removed and sent for testing.

      Should moles be removed by laser?

      Not if there is any doubt about them. Suspicious moles should be cut out and tested in a lab, which laser removal doesn’t allow.
