      Muntah (vomiting) and loya (nausea): causes, muntah darah, medicines and when to see a doctor

      Muntah is the Malay word for vomiting, and loya means feeling sick (nausea). Most vomiting is caused by a stomach bug and stops within 1 to 2 days. The main jobs are to keep sipping fluids and to know the warning signs. Vomiting blood, green vomit and vomiting with a stiff neck or severe tummy pain always need a doctor straight away.

      Key points
      • Vomiting usually stops within 1 to 2 days. Take small, frequent sips of oral rehydration salts, keep breastfeeding, and eat when you feel able to.
      • Vomiting blood (muntah darah) always needs medical help. Call 999 or go to an emergency department if you also feel faint, dizzy, confused or cold and clammy, or have black stools.
      • Green or yellow-green vomit can mean a blocked bowel and needs hospital care, even if the person doesn’t seem dehydrated.
      • Anti-sickness medicines aren’t routinely recommended for children with a stomach bug. Domperidone shouldn’t be used under 12 years, and metoclopramide is not for babies under 1 year.
      • Get urgent help for vomiting with a stiff neck, severe headache or tummy pain, after a head injury, or with dengue warning signs.

      What causes vomiting?

      Common causes:

      • a stomach bug (gastroenteritis) or food poisoning, often with diarrhoea
      • medicines, including strong painkillers and chemotherapy
      • alcohol
      • motion sickness and vertigo
      • migraine
      • early pregnancy

      Serious causes include appendicitis, a blocked bowel, pancreatitis, meningitis, a head injury or bleeding in the brain, diabetic ketoacidosis, and liver or kidney failure. Sudden nausea with heavy chest pain, pain spreading to the arm, jaw or back, or breathlessness can be a heart attack. Call 999.

      In dengue: vomiting more than 3 times in 24 hours is a warning sign. See our guide to platelets and dengue.

      Muntah darah: vomiting blood

      Vomiting blood always needs medical help. The vomit may be bright red, brown or black, or look like coffee grounds. Coffee-ground vomit is blood that has been partly digested by stomach acid, which usually means the bleeding has slowed or stopped. Bright red blood suggests faster bleeding that may still be going on.

      Call 999 or go to an emergency department if you vomit blood and also:

      • feel faint, dizzy or confused
      • are breathing fast
      • have cold, clammy or pale skin
      • have tummy pain
      • have black, tarry stools

      Don’t drive yourself. If the bleeding has stopped and you have no other symptoms, get an urgent appointment with a doctor the same day.

      Common causes:

      • inflammation of the stomach lining (gastritis), stomach ulcers and reflux damage. See our guides to gastric pain and GERD.
      • aspirin, ibuprofen and other anti-inflammatory painkillers, and blood thinners
      • a tear in the food pipe after forceful retching (a Mallory-Weiss tear)
      • swollen veins in the food pipe (varices) in people with liver disease, which can bleed heavily
      • less often, cancer or a bleeding disorder

      Not always from the stomach: blood swallowed during a nosebleed can be vomited, and a breastfed baby can swallow blood from a cracked nipple. In hospital, doctors may use a camera test (endoscopy) to find and treat the bleeding, and give acid-reducing medicines.

      Home care for vomiting

      • Take small, frequent sips. Oral rehydration salts (ORS) replace both water and salts. For a child, start with about 1 teaspoon (5ml) every 5 minutes, and increase slowly if they keep it down. See our diarrhoea guide for how to mix ORS.
      • Avoid fizzy drinks, sports drinks and undiluted juice, especially for children.
      • Babies: keep breastfeeding, with smaller and more frequent feeds. Never make formula weaker than the instructions say.
      • Food: go back to your normal diet once you are keeping fluids down. Plain food is easier at first, and it’s best to avoid fatty or spicy food.
      • Stay off work or school for 48 hours after the last episode of vomiting if it was caused by a stomach bug, to avoid spreading it. Wash your hands with soap and water.

      Ubat muntah: anti-sickness medicines

      Most vomiting from a stomach bug settles without medicine. If needed, a doctor may prescribe an anti-sickness medicine.

      • Children with gastroenteritis: the 2026 Malaysian children’s guideline doesn’t recommend routine anti-sickness medicines. It says domperidone, metoclopramide and dimenhydrinate don’t work well enough and cause side effects. A single dose of ondansetron may be considered by a doctor for persistent vomiting in children from 6 months.
      • Domperidone: Malaysia’s drug regulator, NPRA, says it shouldn’t be used in children under 12 years or under 35kg. It can cause dangerous heart rhythm problems, so it isn’t suitable for people with heart disease or taking certain other medicines. In Europe, it is limited to a maximum of 30mg a day, usually for no more than a week.
      • Metoclopramide: must not be given to babies under 1 year. NPRA has warned about a reaction in which the eyes roll upward uncontrollably (oculogyric crisis), with 241 reports in Malaysia. In children over 1, it is only a second-line choice for limited uses.
      • Adults: doctors may use prochlorperazine, promethazine or metoclopramide for mild to moderate vomiting, and ondansetron for severe vomiting or vomiting caused by chemotherapy.
      • Motion sickness: tablets such as dimenhydrinate or hyoscine, taken before the journey, can help. Ginger may help some people.

      Ask a pharmacist or doctor before taking any anti-sickness medicine, especially for a child, in pregnancy, or if you have a heart condition.

      Vomiting in babies and children

      A stomach bug is the most common cause. Get medical help urgently if a child:

      • has green or yellow-green vomit, or blood in the vomit
      • is a baby aged about 3 to 6 weeks who has forceful (projectile) vomiting after every feed, stays hungry and isn’t gaining weight. This can be pyloric stenosis, a narrowing at the stomach exit.
      • has a bulging soft spot on the head, a stiff neck, a rash or a high fever
      • is unusually sleepy, floppy or crying in a way that can’t be settled
      • has a swollen tummy, or vomiting without diarrhoea that keeps going
      • has vomited more than 6 to 8 times, or for more than 24 to 48 hours
      • hasn’t passed urine or has refused to drink for more than 8 hours

      See our guide to fever in children.

      Vomiting in pregnancy

      Morning sickness is common and usually settles by 16 to 20 weeks. Rest, small frequent meals, plain starchy snacks and sips of fluid can help. Ask a pharmacist before taking supplements, including ginger. A doctor can prescribe anti-sickness medicines that are considered safe in pregnancy.

      Hyperemesis gravidarum is severe vomiting in pregnancy, affecting 1 to 3 in 100 women. It often needs fluids through a drip in hospital.

      See a doctor if you are pregnant and:

      • can’t keep food or fluids down for 24 hours
      • have dark urine, or haven’t passed urine for 8 hours
      • feel faint, or have tummy pain or a fever
      • are losing weight, or vomit blood

      When to get urgent help

      Call 999 or go to an emergency department for vomiting with:

      • blood, or vomit that looks like coffee grounds
      • green vomit, in adults or children
      • a stiff neck, a sudden severe headache or a rash that doesn’t fade
      • sudden or severe tummy pain
      • confusion or unusual drowsiness
      • chest pain or breathlessness

      Get urgent advice the same day if:

      • you have vomited after a head injury
      • you can’t keep fluids down, or vomiting has lasted more than 2 days
      • you have signs of dehydration, such as dark urine, passing little urine and dizziness
      • you have diabetes and are vomiting, especially if your ketones are high. Ask your doctor about a sick day plan.
      • you have a fever in dengue season and are vomiting more than 3 times a day

      Repeated bouts: attacks of vomiting that come back again and again with normal health in between can be cyclic vomiting syndrome, which is often linked to migraine. Regular cannabis use can also cause repeated vomiting that only stops when the person stops using it.

      Frequently asked questions

      What is muntah in English?

      Muntah means vomiting, or being sick. Loya means nausea, the feeling that you might be sick.

      Is vomiting blood an emergency?

      It always needs medical help. Call 999 or go to an emergency department if you also feel faint, dizzy or confused, or have black stools. Otherwise, see a doctor the same day.

      What does coffee-ground vomit mean?

      It is blood that has been partly digested by stomach acid, often from a stomach ulcer or gastritis. It needs medical help the same day.

      What should I drink after vomiting?

      Small, frequent sips of oral rehydration salts. For children, start with a teaspoon every 5 minutes. Avoid fizzy drinks and sports drinks.

      Can I give my child medicine for vomiting?

      Anti-sickness medicines aren’t routinely recommended for children with a stomach bug. Domperidone shouldn’t be used under 12 years. Ask a doctor if vomiting persists.

      What does green vomit mean?

      Green or yellow-green vomit can mean a blocked bowel, especially in babies and children. It needs urgent hospital assessment.

      How long does vomiting last?

      Vomiting from a stomach bug usually stops within 1 to 2 days. Get advice if it lasts longer, or if you can’t keep fluids down.
