      Platelet count (bacaan platelet): normal range, low platelets and what it means in dengue

      Platelets are the blood cells that help your blood clot. A normal count is roughly 150,000 to 400,000 per microlitre, although lab ranges vary. In Malaysia, a falling platelet count is best known as a sign of dengue. But in dengue, the platelet number alone doesn’t predict how ill you will get. Here is how to read the result and what matters more.

      Key points
      • A normal platelet count is roughly 150,000 to 400,000 per microlitre (shown as 150 to 400 ×10⁹/L), although ranges vary between labs.
      • Serious spontaneous bleeding is unusual until platelets fall below about 20,000. Above 50,000, the bleeding risk is minimal.
      • In dengue, platelets usually fall around day 3 to 5 of fever, often as the fever settles. Malaysia’s guideline says the platelet count doesn’t match how severe the illness is. A rising haematocrit with falling platelets is the warning sign.
      • An NS1 test works best in the first 5 days of fever. IgM antibodies usually show up from day 5, so an early negative IgM doesn’t rule dengue out.
      • Papaya leaf, crab soup, isotonic drinks and similar remedies have no robust evidence of changing the course of dengue. Fluids and monitoring are what count.

      What is a normal platelet count?

      Platelets are tiny cells made in the bone marrow. They stick to damaged blood vessels and clump together to form clots. Each platelet lives about 7 to 10 days.

      A normal count is roughly 150,000 to 400,000 per microlitre (µL) of blood. Lab reports often write this as 150 to 400 ×10⁹/L, so a result of “90” means 90,000. Reference ranges differ slightly between labs, so compare your result with the range printed on your report.

      Platelet count (per µL)What it generally means
      Above about 450,000High. Usually a reaction to infection, inflammation, bleeding or low iron
      About 150,000 to 400,000Normal
      50,000 to 150,000Low, with minimal bleeding risk
      20,000 to 50,000Minor bleeding is possible after an injury
      Below 20,000Spontaneous bleeding is possible
      Below 5,000Risk of severe, possibly life-threatening bleeding

      Platelet results are one part of a full blood count, and your doctor will read them alongside your other results and how you are.

      Causes of a low platelet count (platelet rendah)

      A low platelet count is called thrombocytopenia. Dengue is a common cause in Malaysia, but there are many others:

      • Other viral infections, including hepatitis C, HIV, glandular fever (EBV) and flu
      • Medicines, such as heparin, quinine, co-trimoxazole, rifampicin and vancomycin. A few vaccines can occasionally cause a temporary drop.
      • Immune thrombocytopenia (ITP), where the immune system destroys platelets
      • Pregnancy: a mild drop late in pregnancy (gestational thrombocytopenia) affects about 4 to 12 in 100 pregnancies. It usually stays above 70,000, is harmless to the baby, and resolves within about 6 weeks of birth.
      • Liver disease, where an enlarged spleen holds on to platelets, and heavy alcohol use
      • Low vitamin B12 or folate
      • Bone marrow conditions, including leukaemia, lymphoma and aplastic anaemia, and chemotherapy

      A false low result: in about 1 in 1,000 samples, platelets clump together in the collection tube and the machine undercounts them. This is called pseudothrombocytopenia. A blood film or a repeat test in a different tube confirms it, and it needs no treatment.

      Platelets in dengue

      Malaysia’s clinical practice guideline on dengue in adults describes a typical pattern:

      1. Early fever: the white cell count and platelets are usually normal.
      2. The white cell count falls first, then platelets start to fall.
      3. The critical phase often begins after day 3 of fever, or as the fever comes down, and lasts 24 to 48 hours. This is when the blood vessels can leak fluid, and when platelets are usually lowest.
      4. In recovery, platelets rise again.

      Why the platelet number isn’t everything: the guideline states that there is no correlation between disease severity and platelet count, and that a low count doesn’t predict bleeding. What doctors watch most closely is the haematocrit (HCT), which rises when fluid leaks out of the blood vessels. A rising haematocrit together with a rapid fall in platelets is a warning sign.

      • Monitoring: adults with dengue are usually reviewed daily from day 3 of illness until they have been free of fever for 24 to 48 hours without fever medicine. Children have a daily full blood count until the critical phase is over.
      • Children: the Malaysian children’s guideline treats a platelet count falling to 100,000 or below, with a 20% rise in haematocrit, as a warning sign.
      • Platelet transfusion: the guidelines say there is no role for routine preventive platelet transfusion in dengue. It is reserved for situations such as surgery or invasive procedures.

      Dengue tests: NS1, IgM and IgG

      TestBest timeGood to know
      NS1 antigenDays 1 to 5 of feverPicks up 75 to 97% of first infections, but only 60 to 70% of repeat infections. Less reliable from day 4 to 5.
      IgM antibodyFrom day 5Positive in about 80% after day 5 and 93 to 99% from days 6 to 10. A negative result before day 5 to 7 doesn’t rule dengue out.
      IgG antibodyIf IgM is still negative after day 7Can suggest a past or repeat infection
      Combined rapid testAs soon as dengue is suspectedNS1 with IgM and IgG together picks up about 93% of cases. Done with a full blood count.

      A positive test confirms dengue, but a negative early test doesn’t always rule it out. If you still have symptoms, follow your doctor’s advice on repeat tests and daily blood counts.

      Papaya leaf, crab soup and other remedies

      Malaysia’s adult dengue guideline lists papaya leaf, cactus, crab or padi frog soup, bitter gourd, porcupine bezoar, tawa-tawa, pegaga and commercial isotonic drinks as remedies without robust evidence. It notes that changing the platelet count alone doesn’t alter the course of dengue.

      In 2016, the Ministry of Health said local studies found platelets rose after 3 days of papaya leaf juice, and that the juice wasn’t toxic. But ministry hospitals don’t provide it, and it doesn’t treat the leaking blood vessels that make dengue dangerous.

      What helps: drinking enough. The Malaysian guideline advises more than 2 litres a day of fluids such as milk, juice, oral rehydration salts and barley water, not plain water alone.

      Avoid:

      • aspirin, mefenamic acid, ibuprofen and other anti-inflammatory painkillers, and steroids
      • injections, including injections into the muscle
      • massage and cupping
      • antibiotics, which don’t work against dengue

      Use paracetamol for fever, with no more than 4g a day for adults. See our guide to painkillers and fever in adults.

      When to go to hospital

      Go to a hospital emergency department straight away, or call 999, if someone with dengue or a low platelet count has:

      • red spots or patches on the skin, nosebleeds or bleeding gums
      • vomiting blood, or black stools
      • unusually heavy periods or vaginal bleeding
      • vomiting or diarrhoea more than 3 times in 24 hours
      • tummy pain
      • drowsiness, restlessness or irritability
      • pale, cold or clammy skin
      • difficulty breathing

      Watch most closely as the fever comes down, because that is when dengue can become dangerous. If you have a low platelet count without a clear cause, such as dengue, see a doctor to look for the reason.

      Frequently asked questions

      What is a normal platelet count?

      Roughly 150,000 to 400,000 per microlitre, written as 150 to 400 ×10⁹/L on many reports. Lab ranges vary slightly.

      What platelet count is dangerous?

      Serious spontaneous bleeding becomes possible below about 20,000, and the risk is highest below 5,000. Above 50,000, the bleeding risk is minimal.

      What platelet count needs hospital admission in dengue?

      There isn’t one number. Malaysian guidelines look at warning signs and a rising haematocrit with falling platelets, rather than the platelet count alone.

      When do platelets drop in dengue?

      Usually around day 3 to 5 of fever, often as the fever starts to come down. They rise again during recovery.

      Does papaya leaf juice help dengue?

      Some local studies found platelets rose, but Malaysia’s guideline says there is no robust evidence that it changes the course of dengue. Fluids and medical monitoring matter more.

      When should I do an NS1 test?

      In the first 5 days of fever. From day 5, an IgM antibody test is more useful.

      Can I have a low platelet count without dengue?

      Yes. Other infections, medicines, immune conditions, pregnancy, liver disease and bone marrow problems can all lower platelets. See a doctor to find the cause.
