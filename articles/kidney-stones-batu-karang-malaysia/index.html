      Kidney stones (batu karang): symptoms, treatment, and how to stop them coming back

      Kidney stones (batu karang) are hard deposits that form when minerals in the urine crystallise. Small stones often pass on their own, but a stone moving down from the kidney can cause severe pain, and a blocked, infected kidney is an emergency. Malaysia’s hot, humid climate makes dehydration, a major cause, very common.

      Key points
      • The typical symptom is severe pain in the side or lower back that comes in waves and spreads to the groin, often with nausea, vomiting or blood in the urine.
      • Go to an emergency department for stone pain with fever or shivering, pain that painkillers don’t control, or if you can’t pass urine.
      • About 3 in 4 stones smaller than 5 mm pass on their own, usually within a few weeks. Larger stones may need shock-wave treatment (ESWL), laser removal through a thin scope, or keyhole surgery.
      • A Sarawak screening study found stones in about 4% of adults, and many people get another within 5 to 10 years.
      • Drinking 2.5 to 3 litres of fluid a day, cutting salt and keeping a normal calcium intake are the most important ways to prevent stones.

      What are kidney stones?

      Kidney stones form when substances in the urine, such as calcium, oxalate and uric acid, become too concentrated and crystallise. They range from grains of sand to several centimetres across.

      In Malaysia: an ultrasound screening of more than 1,000 adults at clinics in Sarawak found kidney stones in about 4%, and 6.4% had had them at some point. Malaysian urologists point to the hot, humid climate and chronic dehydration as major contributors.

      Risk factors include not drinking enough, a family history of stones, being overweight, diabetes, gout, high blood pressure, bowel disease, repeated urinary infections and some medicines.

      Symptoms of kidney stones (tanda batu karang)

      Stones sitting in the kidney often cause no symptoms. Pain starts when a stone moves into the tube between the kidney and the bladder (the ureter):

      • severe pain in the side or lower back, below the ribs, that comes in waves and may spread to the lower tummy or groin (renal colic)
      • restlessness: people often can’t lie still
      • nausea and vomiting
      • blood in the urine, which may look pink, red or brown
      • needing to pass urine often, or burning when you do

      Go to an emergency department straight away if you have:

      • stone pain with a fever, chills or shivering. This can mean an infected, blocked kidney, which needs urgent drainage and antibiotics.
      • pain that painkillers don’t control, or constant vomiting
      • no urine, or only one working kidney

      Pain in the side with fever but no stone can be a kidney infection: see our guide to urinary tract infections. Severe pain on the lower right side may be appendicitis.

      How kidney stones are diagnosed

      • A CT scan without contrast is the most accurate test and shows the size and position of the stone.
      • An ultrasound is used first in pregnancy and in children, and is often used to check the kidneys for blockage.
      • Urine and blood tests check for blood, infection and kidney function. See our guide to blood test results.

      If you pass a stone, try to catch it by passing urine through a sieve or tea strainer, and give it to your doctor. Knowing what it is made of helps prevent the next one.

      Treatment

      Small stones (under 5 mm): about 3 in 4 pass on their own, and more if they are already low in the ureter. This can take a few weeks.

      • Pain relief: anti-inflammatory painkillers (NSAIDs) are the first choice, as long as your kidneys are working normally and you can take them safely.
      • Drink enough to keep your urine pale, but don’t force down huge amounts.
      • For stones of 5 to 10 mm low in the ureter, a doctor may prescribe a tablet (an alpha-blocker such as tamsulosin) to relax the ureter and help the stone pass. The benefit is modest.

      Larger stones, or stones that don’t pass:

      TreatmentWhat it involvesUsually for
      Shock-wave lithotripsy (ESWL)Shock waves from outside the body break the stone into fragments that pass in the urine. No cutsKidney and some ureteric stones up to about 20 mm
      Ureteroscopy with laser (URS / RIRS)A thin scope is passed up through the urethra and bladder, and a laser breaks up the stone. Usually same-day dischargeUreteric stones, and kidney stones up to about 20 mm
      Percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL)Keyhole surgery through a small cut in the back to remove the stone. A few days in hospitalLarge kidney stones, over 20 mm

      A small tube (stent) is sometimes left between the kidney and bladder for a few weeks after treatment, and removed later. Government hospitals with urology departments, such as Hospital Selayang, offer ESWL and laser treatment by referral.

      Be wary of “ubat batu karang” sold online with claims to dissolve stones. Most stones won’t dissolve, and relying on these can delay treatment while a blocked kidney is damaged. Unregistered products have also been linked to kidney damage. Check for a MAL registration number, and talk to your doctor or pharmacist first.

      How to stop stones coming back

      Stones often come back: by some estimates about half of people get another within 5 to 10 years without prevention. European urology guidelines advise:

      • Drink 2.5 to 3 litres of fluid a day, mainly water, enough to keep your urine pale. Drink more in hot weather, when working outdoors and after exercise.
      • Cut down on salt to 4 to 5 g a day (less than a teaspoon), including salty sauces, processed food and instant noodles.
      • Don’t cut out calcium. Normal calcium intake from food, such as milk, yoghurt and tofu, actually lowers the risk of calcium stones.
      • Moderate animal protein (meat, chicken, seafood).
      • Avoid sugary drinks. Adding fresh lemon or lime juice to water may help.
      • Keep to a healthy weight. See our BMI calculator.
      • Only cut down on high-oxalate foods, such as spinach and nuts, if tests show you have high oxalate levels.

      People who have had several stones, or are at high risk, may be offered urine tests over 24 hours and a stone analysis. Depending on the results, medicines can lower the risk of new stones.

      Kidney stones vs gallstones (batu hempedu)

      Gallstones are a different condition. They form in the gallbladder, under the liver, and cause pain in the upper middle or upper right of the tummy, under the ribs, that can last from 30 minutes to several hours. Yellowing of the skin or eyes with gallstone pain needs urgent care. See our guide to stomach pain.

      Where to get help

      For a first episode of mild stone pain without fever, a GP can examine you, test your urine, give pain relief and arrange a scan or referral to a urologist. Stone pain often strikes at night, so our directory lists clinics open 24 hours and late across Kuala Lumpur, Selangor and other states. For pain with fever, or pain you can’t control, go to an emergency department.

      Frequently asked questions

      What are the symptoms of kidney stones?

      Severe pain in the side or lower back that comes in waves and spreads to the groin, often with nausea, vomiting, blood in the urine or needing to pass urine often.

      Can kidney stones pass on their own?

      Yes, most small ones do. About 3 in 4 stones under 5 mm pass by themselves, usually within a few weeks.

      When is kidney stone pain an emergency?

      If you also have a fever, chills or shivering, if painkillers don’t control the pain, if you are vomiting constantly, or if you can’t pass urine.

      How are large kidney stones removed?

      With shock-wave treatment (ESWL), a thin scope and laser passed up through the urethra (ureteroscopy), or keyhole surgery through the back (PCNL) for large stones.

      How much water should I drink to prevent kidney stones?

      About 2.5 to 3 litres of fluid a day, mostly water, enough to keep your urine pale. More in hot weather or when sweating.

      Should I avoid calcium if I have kidney stones?

      No. A normal calcium intake from food lowers the risk of the most common stones. Cut down on salt instead.

      Do herbal remedies dissolve kidney stones?

      There is no good evidence that they do, and relying on them can delay treatment. Check any product is registered and ask your doctor first.

      What is the difference between kidney stones and gallstones?

      Kidney stones form in the urinary system and cause side or back pain. Gallstones form in the gallbladder and cause pain in the upper right or middle of the tummy.
