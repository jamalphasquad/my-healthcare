      Period pain (senggugut): what’s normal, what helps, and signs of endometriosis or fibroids

      Period pain (senggugut, or dysmenorrhoea) is very common, and for most women it is manageable with the right painkiller taken at the right time, heat and exercise. But period pain that stops you going to school or work, keeps getting worse, or comes with heavy bleeding or pain during sex isn’t something you should just put up with. It can be a sign of a treatable condition such as endometriosis.

      Key points
      • About 3 in 4 secondary schoolgirls in Kuala Lumpur and Kelantan studies had period pain, and more than 1 in 5 of those in Kuala Lumpur missed school because of it. Few saw a doctor.
      • Anti-inflammatory painkillers (NSAIDs) such as ibuprofen, naproxen or mefenamic acid work best, started at the first sign of a period or a day before. They work better than paracetamol.
      • Heat, such as a heat pad or hot water bottle, may work about as well as painkillers. Regular exercise also helps.
      • Hormonal contraception, such as the pill, implant or hormonal coil, can greatly reduce period pain.
      • See a doctor if pain is getting worse, isn’t helped by painkillers, stops you doing daily activities, or comes with heavy periods, bleeding between periods or pain during sex.

      What causes period pain?

      • Primary dysmenorrhoea is ordinary period pain with no underlying disease. It is caused by the womb muscle tightening to shed its lining, driven by chemicals called prostaglandins. It usually starts within a few years of the first period, begins just before or as bleeding starts, and lasts up to 3 days. It often improves with age and after childbirth.
      • Secondary dysmenorrhoea is pain caused by a condition such as endometriosis, adenomyosis, fibroids or pelvic infection. It often starts later in life, gets worse over time, or lasts beyond the first days of bleeding. A copper coil can also make periods more painful in the first few months.

      In Malaysia: in a study of more than 1,000 secondary schoolgirls in Kuala Lumpur, 74.5% had period pain. Of these, 21.5% missed school and about half found it hard to concentrate in class, yet only 12% had seen a doctor. A similar study in rural Kelantan found 76% had period pain.

      What helps period pain (cara hilangkan senggugut)

      Painkillers (ubat senggugut)

      • Anti-inflammatory painkillers (NSAIDs), such as ibuprofen, naproxen or mefenamic acid, block the prostaglandins that cause the pain. A Cochrane review found about half of women got good relief, against 18% with a dummy pill, and NSAIDs worked better than paracetamol.
      • Timing matters: start at the first sign of your period, or a day before if your periods are regular, and take them regularly for the first 2 to 3 days rather than waiting until the pain is bad. Take them with food.
      • Don’t take NSAIDs if you have a stomach ulcer, kidney problems, some types of asthma, or suspected dengue. Paracetamol is an alternative. Ask a pharmacist if unsure.

      Heat: a heat pad, heat patch, hot water bottle or warm bath on the lower tummy or back. A 2026 review of 57 trials found heat may relieve period pain about as well as NSAIDs, with fewer side effects.

      Exercise: regular exercise, such as brisk walking, yoga or aerobics about 3 times a week, reduced period pain in a Cochrane review. Gentle movement during your period can help too.

      Other options: a TENS machine (a small device that sends mild electrical pulses through the skin) may help a little. Ginger has been studied in small trials, with some benefit, but the evidence is weak. Not smoking and cutting down on alcohol can also help.

      Hormonal treatment

      If painkillers and heat aren’t enough, hormonal contraception can make periods lighter and much less painful, and is often a first choice for women who also want contraception:

      • the combined pill, which can also be taken back-to-back to have fewer periods
      • the hormonal coil (IUS), which reduces both pain and heavy bleeding
      • the implant or progestogen-only pill

      See our guide to contraception in Malaysia. A doctor will usually review how you are doing after about 3 months.

      When period pain isn’t “normal”

      See a doctor if:

      • your periods are becoming more painful, heavier or irregular
      • pain stops you going to school or work, or isn’t helped by painkillers taken properly
      • you have pain during or after sex
      • you have pain passing urine or opening your bowels during your period, or bowel or bladder changes
      • you have bleeding between periods or after sex
      • your periods are very heavy: changing a pad every 1 to 2 hours, bleeding for more than 7 days, passing large clots, flooding, or feeling tired and breathless (signs of anaemia)
      • you have had pelvic infections before, have a family history of endometriosis, or are having difficulty getting pregnant

      Get urgent help for sudden, severe pelvic pain, especially with fever, fainting, or if you could be pregnant.

      Endometriosis

      Endometriosis is when tissue similar to the womb lining grows outside the womb, for example on the ovaries and pelvic lining, causing inflammation, pain and scarring.

      • It affects about 1 in 10 women of reproductive age worldwide, and Malaysian specialists give similar estimates.
      • Symptoms include very painful periods, pain during sex, pelvic pain at other times, pain opening the bowels, tiredness, and difficulty getting pregnant.
      • Diagnosis is often delayed by years: worldwide, 4 to 12 years on average. Malaysian patient groups report women being told their pain is normal.
      • A normal ultrasound or MRI doesn’t rule it out. The definite test is a keyhole operation (laparoscopy), which can also remove the tissue.
      • There is no cure, but treatment with painkillers, hormonal medicines and surgery can control symptoms well, and fertility treatment can help women trying to conceive.

      Fibroids and adenomyosis

      • Fibroids are non-cancerous growths in or on the womb. Many cause no symptoms, but they can cause heavy or painful periods, pressure on the bladder or bowel, and iron-deficiency anaemia.
      • Adenomyosis is when the womb lining grows into the muscle wall of the womb. It is more common after 35 and after having children, and causes painful, heavy periods.

      Both are usually found on an ultrasound. Treatment ranges from painkillers, medicine to reduce bleeding and hormonal treatment to procedures and surgery, depending on your symptoms and whether you want children. Heavy periods can cause anaemia: see our guide to blood test results.

      Irregular periods with acne or excess hair may point to PCOS (PMOS) instead.

      Vaginal discharge (keputihan): what’s normal?

      Some vaginal discharge is normal and healthy. It keeps the vagina clean.

      • Normal: clear or white, thin and slippery or thick and sticky, with no strong smell. It changes through your cycle and is often wetter and stretchier around ovulation.
      ChangePossible cause
      Thin, grey-white, fishy smellBacterial vaginosis
      Thick, white, like cottage cheese, with itchingThrush (yeast infection)
      Green, yellow or frothyTrichomoniasis
      With pelvic pain, bleeding or pain passing urineChlamydia or gonorrhoea

      See a doctor if your discharge changes in colour, smell or amount, or comes with itching, soreness, pain or bleeding. Don’t douche or use perfumed washes inside the vagina, which upset its natural balance. See our guides to STI testing, UTIs and the Pap smear and HPV test.

      Period pain, school and work

      Malaysia has no separate law on menstrual leave. The government said in 2022 that period leave would need further study, and no policy has followed. If period pain stops you working, a doctor can assess you and decide whether to issue an MC. See our guide to medical certificates. If you regularly miss school or work because of period pain, that is a reason to see a doctor, as treatment usually helps.

      Where to get period pain checked

      A GP can advise on the right painkiller, start hormonal treatment, check for anaemia, examine you and arrange an ultrasound, and refer you to a gynaecologist if endometriosis or fibroids are suspected.       Frequently asked questions

      What is senggugut in English?

      Senggugut is period pain, also called menstrual cramps or dysmenorrhoea.

      What is the best medicine for period pain?

      Anti-inflammatory painkillers (NSAIDs) such as ibuprofen, naproxen or mefenamic acid, started at the first sign of your period and taken regularly for the first 2 to 3 days. Paracetamol is an alternative if you can’t take NSAIDs.

      How can I relieve period pain without medicine?

      Heat on the lower tummy or back, such as a heat pad or hot water bottle, gentle exercise, a warm bath and massage. Regular exercise through the month also helps.

      Is severe period pain normal?

      Some pain is common, but pain that stops you going to school or work, isn’t helped by painkillers or is getting worse should be checked by a doctor.

      What are the signs of endometriosis?

      Very painful periods, pain during sex, pelvic pain at other times, pain opening your bowels during your period, and difficulty getting pregnant.

      Can the pill help period pain?

      Yes. The combined pill, hormonal coil and implant can make periods lighter and much less painful.

      What colour discharge is normal?

      Clear or white with no strong smell is normal. Grey with a fishy smell, thick and white with itching, or green, yellow or frothy discharge can mean an infection.

      Can I get an MC for period pain in Malaysia?

      There is no separate menstrual leave law. A doctor can assess you and decide whether to issue an MC if pain stops you working.
