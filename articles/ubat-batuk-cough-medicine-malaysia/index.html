      Ubat batuk: which cough medicines work, dry vs chesty cough, children and safety

      Ubat batuk means cough medicine. Most coughs from colds clear up on their own within 3 weeks, and research finds little good evidence that over-the-counter cough medicines speed this up. Some can ease symptoms, but they aren’t safe for everyone. Here is what is in them, what to choose, and what to avoid, especially for children.

      Key points
      • A Cochrane review found no good evidence for or against over-the-counter cough medicines. They may ease a cough a little, but they won’t cure it.
      • Honey is probably better than no treatment for children’s coughs, and works about as well as dextromethorphan. Never give honey to babies under 1 year.
      • In Malaysia, liquid cough and cold medicines are labelled not for children under 2, and should only be used for ages 2 to 6 on a doctor’s or pharmacist’s advice. Codeine is not for children under 12.
      • Codeine cough medicines can only be supplied by a doctor or pharmacist. Misusing cough syrup, including mixing it with ketum, is an offence.
      • Check combination products so you don’t take paracetamol twice, and avoid decongestants such as pseudoephedrine if you have high blood pressure.

      Dry cough or chesty cough?

      Type of medicineMeant forGeneric names
      Cough suppressantDry, tickly cough (batuk kering)Dextromethorphan, codeine
      ExpectorantChesty cough with phlegm (batuk berkahak)Guaifenesin
      Mucolytic (loosens phlegm)Chesty cough with thick phlegmBromhexine, ambroxol, carbocisteine, acetylcysteine
      Sedating antihistamineOften added to night-time mixturesChlorpheniramine, diphenhydramine, promethazine
      DecongestantBlocked nosePseudoephedrine, phenylephrine

      Don’t take a suppressant for a chesty cough, as coughing up phlegm helps clear your chest. Many products combine several of these ingredients, so read the label.

      Do cough medicines work?

      A 2014 Cochrane review of 29 trials with nearly 5,000 people concluded there is no good evidence for or against over-the-counter cough medicines:

      • Adults: results for suppressants were mixed. One of three trials of guaifenesin found a benefit, and so did one mucolytic trial. Antihistamines worked no better than a dummy treatment.
      • Children: suppressants, antihistamines and antihistamine-decongestant mixtures worked no better than a dummy treatment.

      The NHS says cough syrups “may help you cough less” but won’t stop a cough, and that decongestants and codeine cough medicines won’t stop one either. A cough after a cold commonly lasts up to 3 weeks.

      Honey: a 2018 Cochrane review found honey is probably better than no treatment for children’s coughs, works about as well as dextromethorphan, and may work better than diphenhydramine. Try a teaspoon in warm water or on its own. Don’t give honey to babies under 1 year, because of the risk of botulism.

      Steam: breathing in steam hasn’t been shown to help, and steam bowls can scald children.

      Cough medicines for children

      • Under 2 years: Malaysia’s drug authority requires liquid cough and cold medicines containing antihistamines, cough suppressants or decongestants to be labelled “Not to be used in children below two years of age”.
      • 2 to 6 years: use these only with caution, on the advice of a doctor or pharmacist. The UK advises no cough and cold medicines at all under 6.
      • Codeine: Malaysia’s labelling rules say codeine mustn’t be used for coughs and colds in children under 12, isn’t recommended from 12 to 18 for those with breathing problems, and mustn’t be used by anyone under 18 after tonsil or adenoid removal for sleep apnoea.
      • Sedating antihistamines such as promethazine can cause dangerous breathing problems in young children.
      • Mucolytics: acetylcysteine and carbocisteine shouldn’t be given to children under 2. In France, some babies produced more mucus instead of less.
      • Use the measuring device provided, and don’t give two products containing the same ingredient.

      For most children, rest, fluids and honey (from age 1) are enough. See our guides to colds (selsema) and fever in children.

      Codeine cough syrup: Malaysian rules

      Codeine is a controlled medicine under Malaysia’s Poisons Act. Codeine cough products can only be supplied by a doctor or a pharmacist. The Ministry of Health has said cough medicines containing scheduled poisons can be sold only for medical purposes.

      Codeine and some other cough syrups have been misused in Malaysia, for example mixed with ketum and fizzy drinks. In 2018, the ministry reported seizing more than 100,000 bottles, and the penalty for illegal sale includes a fine of up to RM3,000, up to a year in prison, or both.

      Codeine can:

      • cause marked drowsiness, so don’t drive or drink alcohol
      • slow breathing dangerously, especially in children and in people who break it down unusually fast
      • pass into breast milk, so Malaysian labelling says not to use it while breastfeeding
      • be habit-forming

      The US FDA has limited codeine cough medicines to adults aged 18 and over. Malaysia has also cancelled 14 pholcodine products, after links to severe allergic reactions during general anaesthesia. If you have taken pholcodine in the past, tell your surgical team before an operation.

      Safety tips: combinations, blood pressure and pregnancy

      • Paracetamol twice: many cold and flu products already contain paracetamol. Don’t take extra paracetamol on top. See our guide to painkillers.
      • Decongestants: pseudoephedrine is in 62 registered combination products in Malaysia. NPRA warns of rare but serious brain blood vessel problems. Avoid it with severe or uncontrolled high blood pressure or kidney disease. Stop and get help for a sudden severe headache, confusion, seizures or vision changes. Check with a pharmacist first if you have heart disease, diabetes, glaucoma, thyroid problems or an enlarged prostate.
      • Antidepressants: dextromethorphan can interact with some antidepressants and cause serotonin syndrome. Ask a pharmacist.
      • Drowsiness: sedating antihistamines and codeine can make you sleepy. Don’t drive or operate machinery.
      • Pregnancy: check with a doctor or pharmacist before taking any cough medicine.
      • Breastfeeding: guaifenesin is probably fine. Avoid codeine, and note that decongestant tablets may reduce milk supply.

      Check that a cough product is registered

      Registered medicines in Malaysia carry a MAL number (MAL followed by 8 digits and a letter) and a hologram security label. The last letter tells you the type:

      • A: controlled medicine
      • X: over-the-counter medicine
      • T: traditional product, such as herbal cough remedies
      • N: health supplement

      You can check a product by name or registration number on NPRA’s QUEST3+ product search. Registration of a traditional product doesn’t mean it has been proven to work.

      Kahak: does green phlegm mean I need antibiotics?

      No. In a study of more than 3,400 adults with a cough, people with yellow or green phlegm were given antibiotics more often, but the antibiotics didn’t help them recover faster. Most coughs are caused by viruses, which antibiotics don’t treat. For bronchitis, antibiotics shortened the cough by only about half a day, with more side effects.

      See a doctor if you have:

      • a cough lasting more than 3 weeks. See our guide to persistent cough.
      • blood in your phlegm
      • breathlessness, chest pain or wheezing. See our guide to asthma.
      • a fever lasting more than 5 days
      • weight loss, night sweats or a long-lasting cough, which can be signs of tuberculosis

      Babies: whooping cough in babies under 3 months may not cause a whoop. Call 999 if a baby turns blue or grey or struggles to breathe, and get urgent help for any baby under 6 months with a bad cough.

      Frequently asked questions

      What is the best ubat batuk?

      There is no clear winner. For a dry cough, dextromethorphan may help a little. For a chesty cough, guaifenesin may help. Honey is a good option for children over 1. Ask a pharmacist.

      Which cough medicine is for batuk berkahak?

      An expectorant such as guaifenesin, or a mucolytic such as bromhexine or carbocisteine. Avoid cough suppressants for a chesty cough.

      Can I give cough syrup to my baby?

      No. In Malaysia, liquid cough and cold medicines are labelled not for children under 2. Don’t give honey to babies under 1 year either. See a doctor if a baby has a cough.

      Is codeine cough syrup available without a prescription?

      Codeine is controlled and can only be supplied by a doctor or pharmacist. It isn’t for children under 12 and shouldn’t be used while breastfeeding.

      Does green phlegm mean I need antibiotics?

      No. Green or yellow phlegm is common with viral infections, and antibiotics don’t speed recovery in most cases.

      Does honey help a cough?

      Yes, probably. Studies in children found honey better than no treatment and about as good as dextromethorphan. Don’t give it to babies under 1.

      How do I check if a cough medicine is registered?

      Look for a MAL number and hologram on the pack, then search the product on NPRA’s QUEST3+ website.
