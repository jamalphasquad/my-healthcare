      Rotavirus in babies and children: symptoms, dehydration warning signs, and the rotavirus vaccine

      Rotavirus is a highly contagious virus that causes vomiting and watery diarrhoea in babies and young children. Most recover at home within a week, but it can quickly cause dehydration. An oral vaccine given in the first months of life prevents most severe cases. It isn’t yet part of Malaysia’s free National Immunisation Programme, so parents get it at private clinics and hospitals.

      Key points
      • Rotavirus is the leading cause of severe, dehydrating diarrhoea in children under 5. It is most severe between about 6 months and 2 years.
      • Symptoms are vomiting, frequent watery diarrhoea, fever and tummy pain, usually lasting 3 to 8 days.
      • The main danger is dehydration. Keep breastfeeding or formula feeding, and give oral rehydration solution in small, frequent sips.
      • The vaccine is given as drops by mouth in 2 or 3 doses, starting at about 2 months. The first dose must be given before 15 weeks and all doses before 8 months.
      • It isn’t in Malaysia’s National Immunisation Programme. Ask your clinic early, as the age limits are strict.

      What is rotavirus?

      Rotavirus infects the gut and causes gastroenteritis (a stomach bug). The World Health Organization calls it the leading cause of severe, dehydrating diarrhoea in children under 5 worldwide.

      • It spreads very easily, through tiny amounts of stool on hands, toys, surfaces and food. Children can pass it on before they have symptoms and for days after they recover, and the virus survives well on surfaces.
      • Who gets it: almost all children catch it at least once by age 5. The most severe illness is in unvaccinated children aged about 3 months to 3 years, and Malaysian health officials have said those aged 6 months to 2 years are most vulnerable.

      In Malaysia, a 2016 study estimated that rotavirus causes about 31,000 hospital admissions, 41,000 outpatient visits and 27 deaths each year in children under 5. In hospitals in Kuala Lumpur and Kuala Terengganu, 38% of tested stool samples from young children admitted with gastroenteritis were positive for rotavirus, and the costs were a heavy burden for many families.

      Symptoms

      Symptoms usually start about 2 days after exposure:

      • sudden vomiting, often first
      • frequent, watery diarrhoea
      • fever
      • tummy pain and loss of appetite

      Vomiting and diarrhoea usually last 3 to 8 days. Other viruses and bacteria cause similar illness. See our guide to food poisoning and gastroenteritis.

      Caring for your child at home

      • Keep breastfeeding, or keep giving formula made up at its usual strength, in smaller, more frequent feeds.
      • Give oral rehydration solution (ORS) from a pharmacy, mixed exactly as the pack says, in small sips every few minutes, especially after each vomit or loose stool. A spoon or syringe can help.
      • Avoid fruit juice and fizzy drinks, which can make diarrhoea worse.
      • Offer food once your child wants to eat.
      • Don’t give anti-diarrhoea medicine to children. Paracetamol can help with fever and discomfort. See our guide to fever in children.

      Dehydration warning signs

      See a doctor urgently if your child:

      • has fewer wet nappies or passes little urine
      • has a dry mouth, cries with few or no tears, or has sunken eyes
      • in babies, has a sunken soft spot (fontanelle) on the head
      • is drowsy, irritable, or drinking poorly
      • can’t keep fluids down, or has been vomiting for more than 2 days
      • has blood in the stool, or green vomit
      • is a young baby, especially under 6 months

      Go to an emergency department if your child is very drowsy or hard to wake, is breathing fast, or has pale, blotchy, blue or cold skin. These are signs of severe dehydration. See our guide on clinic or emergency department.

      The rotavirus vaccine

      The rotavirus vaccine is given as drops into the mouth, not an injection. There are two types:

      • a 2-dose vaccine, usually at about 2 and 4 months
      • a 3-dose vaccine, usually at about 2, 4 and 6 months

      Timing is strict: the first dose should be given before 15 weeks of age, and all doses by 8 months. If your baby misses these windows, they can’t start the course later, so plan ahead with your clinic.

      How well it works: the vaccine prevents about 9 in 10 cases of severe rotavirus illness in countries like the US, with somewhat lower protection in some lower-income settings. It doesn’t prevent every bout of diarrhoea, but makes severe illness and hospital stays much less likely.

      Safety: it is very safe. Mild fussiness or loose stools can follow. There is a very small risk of intussusception, where part of the bowel folds into itself, estimated at about 1 in 20,000 to 1 in 100,000 babies, mostly in the week after a dose. The World Health Organization says the benefits far outweigh this risk. See a doctor urgently if, after a dose, your baby has bouts of severe crying, pulls their legs up to the chest, vomits repeatedly or has blood in the stool.

      The vaccine virus is passed in the stool for a few weeks afterwards, so wash your hands well after nappy changes.

      The rotavirus vaccine in Malaysia

      • The rotavirus vaccine is not part of Malaysia’s National Immunisation Programme, so it isn’t given free at Klinik Kesihatan. Parents usually get it at private clinics and hospitals.
      • Malaysian paediatricians have long called for it to be added to the national programme, and a university workshop in August 2026 highlighted the gap.
      • Ask your clinic about it at your baby’s 2-month check, alongside the routine vaccines. See our baby vaccination schedule.

      Stopping it spreading

      • Wash hands well with soap and water after nappy changes and toilet use, and before preparing food.
      • Clean toys, changing areas and toilet surfaces often.
      • Keep children home from nursery or school until 2 days after the diarrhoea and vomiting have stopped.
      • Don’t share towels, cups or utensils during the illness.

      Handwashing helps, but because rotavirus spreads so easily, vaccination is the best protection.

      Where to get the vaccine or see a doctor

      Many private GP and children’s clinics offer the rotavirus vaccine, and can check a child with vomiting and diarrhoea for dehydration. Our directory lists GP clinics across Kuala Lumpur, Selangor and other states, including clinics open 24 hours. Call ahead to check the vaccine is in stock.

      Frequently asked questions

      What are the symptoms of rotavirus?

      Sudden vomiting, frequent watery diarrhoea, fever and tummy pain, usually lasting 3 to 8 days.

      How long is rotavirus contagious?

      From before symptoms start until a few days after recovery. Keep children home until 2 days after the diarrhoea and vomiting stop.

      How do I treat rotavirus at home?

      Keep breastfeeding or formula feeding, give oral rehydration solution in small, frequent sips, and watch for dehydration. Don’t give anti-diarrhoea medicine to children.

      When should I take my child to hospital?

      If they are very drowsy, have far fewer wet nappies, can’t keep fluids down, have sunken eyes or a sunken soft spot, blood in the stool, or cold, pale or blotchy skin.

      Is the rotavirus vaccine free in Malaysia?

      No. It isn’t part of the National Immunisation Programme, so it is available at private clinics and hospitals.

      When should my baby get the rotavirus vaccine?

      Usually at 2 and 4 months, or 2, 4 and 6 months depending on the vaccine. The first dose must be before 15 weeks and all doses by 8 months.

      Is the rotavirus vaccine safe?

      Yes. There is a very small risk of intussusception, a bowel problem, mostly in the week after a dose. Experts agree the benefits far outweigh this.

      Can my child get rotavirus more than once?

      Yes, but later infections are usually milder, and vaccination makes severe illness much less likely.
