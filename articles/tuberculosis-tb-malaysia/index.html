      Tuberculosis (TB, tibi, batuk kering) in Malaysia: symptoms, tests, treatment and latent TB

      Tuberculosis (TB, tibi or batuk kering) is a bacterial infection that usually affects the lungs and spreads through the air. It is curable, but Malaysia still records more than 26,000 cases and 2,500 deaths a year. Clusters in Johor in early 2026 brought it back into the news. If you have a cough lasting more than 2 weeks, get checked.

      Key points
      • Malaysia recorded 26,183 TB cases and 2,580 deaths in 2024. About 85% of cases are in Malaysians, and Sabah, Selangor and Sarawak have the most.
      • See a doctor for a cough lasting more than 2 weeks, especially with fever, night sweats, weight loss or coughing up blood.
      • TB spreads through the air during close, repeated contact. It doesn’t spread through food, drink, handshakes or kissing.
      • Standard treatment is 6 months of daily tablets. Stopping early can lead to relapse and drug-resistant TB. Most people stop being infectious after about 2 weeks of effective treatment.
      • Latent TB means you carry the bacteria without being ill or infectious. Close contacts should be checked, and some are offered preventive treatment.

      TB in Malaysia

      • In 2024, Malaysia reported 26,183 cases and 2,580 deaths, about 77 cases per 100,000 people. The government aims to eliminate TB by 2035.
      • Up to 6 June 2026, there were 11,257 cases, 4.4% more than the same period in 2025. The Health Minister attributed the rise to more active case-finding.
      • In February 2026, a cluster of 37 cases in Kota Tinggi, Johor, including students, led to more than 800 contacts being screened and a wave of public concern.
      • In the first weeks of 2026, Sabah had the most cases, followed by Selangor, Sarawak, Johor and Kuala Lumpur.
      • About 85% of cases are in Malaysians, and about 15% in foreigners. Health officials call it a disease of poverty and crowding, and urge people not to shun patients.

      How TB spreads, and who is at risk

      TB is caused by the bacterium Mycobacterium tuberculosis. Someone with active TB in the lungs releases it into the air when they cough, sneeze or speak. It spreads more slowly than flu or COVID-19, usually through prolonged, repeated contact in shared indoor air, such as at home, in hostels or in crowded workplaces.

      TB doesn’t spread through food or drink, toothbrushes, handshakes or kissing, and only people with active TB can pass it on.

      Higher risk: people with diabetes, HIV, poor nutrition, those who smoke or drink heavily, close contacts of someone with TB, and young children. Diabetes matters especially in Malaysia: in a Kelantan study, 28% of TB patients had diabetes, and they were about 3 times more likely to have poor treatment outcomes. That is why every TB patient is screened for diabetes and HIV. See our guide to blood sugar.

      Symptoms of TB

      • a cough lasting more than 2 weeks
      • coughing up blood or phlegm
      • fever and night sweats
      • loss of appetite and weight loss
      • tiredness and chest pain

      TB can also affect other parts of the body, causing swollen glands, joint or back pain, or headaches and confusion if it reaches the brain. In children, it may show as poor weight gain and growth. Some people with TB have few or no symptoms but can still spread it.

      For other causes of a lingering cough, see our guide to a cough that won’t go away.

      Tests for TB

      • Sputum tests: two or more samples of phlegm, ideally including an early-morning one, are examined and tested. Molecular tests (Xpert Ultra) used in Ministry of Health facilities find TB quickly and detect some drug resistance.
      • Chest X-ray
      • Culture and drug-sensitivity tests show which medicines will work.
      • Tuberculin skin test (Mantoux) and IGRA blood test show whether you have been infected. They can’t tell latent from active TB, and may stay negative in the first 8 weeks after exposure.

      TB is a notifiable disease under the Prevention and Control of Infectious Diseases Act 1988, so doctors must report cases to the health office, which then traces close contacts.

      Treatment

      Under Malaysia’s clinical practice guideline, standard treatment for lung TB takes 6 months:

      • First 2 months: four medicines daily (isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide and ethambutol)
      • Next 4 months: two medicines daily (isoniazid and rifampicin)

      TB of the brain, bones or joints needs longer treatment. Drug-resistant TB can take up to 2 years.

      • Directly observed treatment (DOT): a health worker, trained family member or volunteer watches you take your medicines. Video DOT is available in some places.
      • Finish the full course, even when you feel better. Many people feel well after a few weeks, but stopping early can cause relapse and drug-resistant TB. Researchers report that a substantial share of Malaysian patients don’t complete treatment.
      • Side effects: orange or red urine and tears are harmless. See a doctor quickly for yellow eyes or skin, dark urine, vomiting, rash, numbness, or changes in your vision.
      • Cost: TB medicines are provided free at government health facilities.
      • Infectiousness: most people stop being infectious after about 2 weeks of effective treatment, but your doctor will decide when it is safe to return to work or school.

      Latent TB

      About a quarter of people worldwide have been infected with TB bacteria. In latent TB, the bacteria are inactive: you feel well, you can’t pass it on, and your chest X-ray is usually normal. About 5 to 10% go on to develop active TB, most often in the first few years, and the risk is higher with a weak immune system, diabetes, or in young children.

      Preventive treatment greatly lowers the risk. Malaysia’s guideline recommends it for groups such as household contacts, people with HIV, people on dialysis or some immune-suppressing medicines, and young children, usually with a 3-month course. Children under 5 who have been in contact with TB are at highest risk and should be checked promptly.

      If someone close to you has TB

      • Go for screening when the health office contacts you, even if you feel well. Tests may be repeated after 8 weeks.
      • Keep rooms well ventilated, with windows open and fans on.
      • The person with TB should cover coughs and wear a mask, especially in the first weeks of treatment.
      • Support them to finish treatment. People with TB aren’t dangerous once treated, and stigma stops people from coming forward.

      The Ministry of Health also advises wearing a mask if you have a cough or are in crowded places.

      BCG vaccine: Malaysian babies get BCG at birth. It protects young children against severe forms of TB, such as TB meningitis. Its protection against lung TB in adults is uncertain, so vaccinated people can still get TB. See our baby vaccination schedule.

      Where to get checked for TB

      If you have a cough lasting more than 2 weeks, a GP or Klinik Kesihatan can arrange a chest X-ray and sputum test and refer you for treatment. Our directory lists GP clinics across Kuala Lumpur, Selangor and other states.

      Frequently asked questions

      What are the symptoms of TB?

      A cough lasting more than 2 weeks, coughing up blood, fever, night sweats, loss of appetite and weight loss.

      How does TB spread?

      Through the air when someone with active lung TB coughs, sneezes or speaks, usually after prolonged contact. Not through food, drink, handshakes or kissing.

      Is TB curable?

      Yes. Standard TB is cured with 6 months of medicines, as long as the full course is finished.

      How long is TB treatment?

      Usually 6 months for lung TB: four medicines for 2 months, then two medicines for 4 months. Drug-resistant TB can take up to 2 years.

      When is someone with TB no longer infectious?

      Usually after about 2 weeks of effective treatment, but a doctor decides when it is safe to return to work or school.

      Is TB treatment free in Malaysia?

      TB medicines are provided free at government health facilities. Check with the clinic about other charges.

      What is latent TB?

      An inactive infection with no symptoms. You can’t spread it, but it can turn into active TB, so some people are offered preventive treatment.

      Does the BCG vaccine prevent TB?

      It protects young children from severe TB, but its protection against lung TB in adults is uncertain.
