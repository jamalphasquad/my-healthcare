      Warts (ketuat): types, treatment at home and at a clinic, and when to see a doctor

      Warts (ketuat, also called kutil) are small, rough growths on the skin caused by the human papillomavirus (HPV). They are very common, especially in children, and are harmless. Many go away by themselves, but treatment can speed things up. Warts on the face or genitals, and anything that bleeds or changes, need a doctor.

      Key points
      • Warts are caused by HPV entering through small breaks in the skin. They spread by touch and on wet surfaces such as shower floors, but aren’t very contagious.
      • Half of children’s warts go away within a year and two-thirds within two years without treatment.
      • Salicylic acid from a pharmacy works if used daily for up to 3 months. Freezing (cryotherapy) at a clinic is another option.
      • Don’t treat warts on the face or genitals yourself, and check with a doctor first if you have diabetes or poor circulation.
      • See a doctor if a growth bleeds, changes, hurts or you aren’t sure it is a wart.

      Types of warts

      TypeWhereWhat it looks like
      Common wartFingers, hands, knees, elbowsFirm, rough, raised bump, often with tiny black dots (clotted blood vessels)
      Plantar wart (verruca)Soles of the feetFlat, hard patch pushed into the skin by walking, often painful, with black dots
      Flat (plane) wartFace, backs of hands, legsSmall, smooth, slightly raised, often in groups. Shaving can spread them
      Filiform wartFace, around the mouth and noseThin, finger-like growth
      Periungual wartAround the fingernails and toenailsRough growths that can affect nail growth
      Genital wartsGenitals and anusSmall, soft lumps. Caused by different HPV types, mostly passed on through sex

      Plantar wart or corn? Plantar warts are sometimes called mata ikan because of their black dots. Corns and calluses are thickened skin from pressure or rubbing, often from tight shoes, and aren’t caused by a virus. If the hard skin is gently pared down, a wart shows tiny black dots while a corn doesn’t.

      How warts spread

      • by direct skin contact, or by touching surfaces such as towels, shoes, and wet floors around pools and showers
      • from one part of your own body to another, for example by picking, biting nails or shaving over warts
      • more easily when the skin is wet, soft or damaged

      A wart can take weeks to months to appear after contact. Children and teenagers get them most, as do people with eczema or a weak immune system.

      Genital warts are a sexually transmitted infection, usually caused by HPV types 6 and 11. They spread through skin-to-skin contact during sex, even when no warts are visible, but not through towels or toilet seats. They need a doctor. See our guide to STI testing.

      Treating warts at home (ubat ketuat)

      Treatment isn’t essential, as many warts go away on their own: half of children’s warts within a year, and two-thirds within two years. If a wart is painful, spreading or bothering you, these options from a pharmacy can help:

      Salicylic acid (gel, paint or plaster) is the best-studied home treatment. In a Cochrane review it worked better than a dummy treatment.

      1. Soak the wart in warm water for a few minutes, then dry it.
      2. Gently rub away the dead surface skin with a pumice stone or emery board. Use it only for the wart, and don’t share it.
      3. Apply the treatment only to the wart, protecting the skin around it, once a day.
      4. Keep going for up to 12 weeks. Stopping early is the commonest reason it fails. Take a few days off if the skin becomes sore.

      Freezing sprays sold in pharmacies are weaker than clinic freezing and aren’t suitable for young children.

      Don’t use home treatments:

      • on the face, genitals or broken skin
      • if you have diabetes, poor circulation or numbness in your feet, without a doctor’s advice

      Never cut, burn or dig out a wart yourself. Be careful with “wart remover” products sold online. Check that the product is registered with the National Pharmaceutical Regulatory Agency (NPRA), and stop if it causes pain, bleeding or blistering.

      Clinic treatments

      TreatmentWhat it involves
      Cryotherapy (freezing)Liquid nitrogen is applied to freeze the wart. It stings and usually blisters, then the wart falls off within a week or so. Several sessions, 2 to 4 weeks apart, are often needed. Not usually used in young children
      Curettage and cauteryThe wart is scraped away under local anaesthetic and the base sealed with heat. Can leave a scar
      LaserFor stubborn warts that haven’t responded to other treatment
      Prescription creams and immunotherapyMedicines that irritate the wart or stimulate the immune system to clear it, mainly for stubborn or genital warts

      Freezing combined with salicylic acid may work better than salicylic acid alone. All treatments can leave small marks or pale or dark patches, and warts can come back. Freezing near the nails can damage the nail.

      Genital warts are treated by a doctor with prescription creams, freezing or other procedures. Don’t use pharmacy wart products on them.

      When to see a doctor

      • the growth bleeds, changes in size, shape or colour, itches or hurts
      • you aren’t sure it is a wart. Some skin cancers and other growths, such as seborrhoeic keratoses, can look similar.
      • it is on your face or genitals, or you have many warts
      • it keeps coming back or spreading, or home treatment hasn’t worked after 12 weeks
      • you have diabetes, poor circulation or a weak immune system

      Preventing warts

      • Don’t pick, scratch or bite warts, and don’t shave over them.
      • Don’t share towels, shoes or socks.
      • Wear flip-flops in shared showers and around pools, and cover verrucas with a waterproof plaster when swimming.
      • Keep your feet dry and change socks daily.
      • Vaccines covering HPV types 6 and 11 protect against most genital warts, and the HPV vaccine also prevents cervical and other cancers. Ask your doctor which vaccine you are being offered. See our guide to the HPV vaccine.

      Where to get warts treated

      Many GP clinics can confirm a wart, freeze or remove it, and refer you to a dermatologist for stubborn or unusual growths. Our directory lists GP clinics across Kuala Lumpur, Selangor and other states. Call ahead to check they offer cryotherapy.

      Frequently asked questions

      What causes ketuat?

      Warts (ketuat) are caused by the human papillomavirus (HPV), which gets into the skin through small cuts or breaks.

      Do warts go away on their own?

      Often, yes. Half of children’s warts disappear within a year and two-thirds within two years. Adults’ warts can take longer.

      What is the best treatment for warts?

      Salicylic acid used every day for up to 12 weeks is the best-studied home treatment. Freezing at a clinic is an alternative, and the two can be combined.

      Are warts contagious?

      Mildly. They spread by touch and on wet surfaces such as shower floors, and you can spread them on your own body by picking or shaving.

      What is the difference between a corn and a plantar wart?

      A corn is thickened skin from pressure. A plantar wart is caused by a virus and shows tiny black dots when the hard skin is pared down.

      Can I remove a wart myself?

      You can treat common warts and verrucas with pharmacy products, but never cut or burn them, and don’t treat warts on the face or genitals yourself.

      Does freezing warts hurt?

      It stings during the treatment and usually blisters afterwards. Several sessions may be needed.

      Are genital warts the same as ordinary warts?

      No. They are caused by different HPV types, are usually sexually transmitted, and need a doctor’s treatment.
