      Warts (ketuat): types, treatment at home and at a clinic, and when to see a doctor

      Warts (ketuat, also called kutil) are small, rough growths caused by the human papillomavirus (HPV). They are very common, especially in children, and harmless. Many disappear without treatment, though treatment can make them go sooner. Warts on the face or genitals, and any growth that bleeds or changes, need a doctor.

      Key points
      • HPV causes warts by getting in through small breaks in the skin. They spread by touch and on wet surfaces such as shower floors, but they aren’t very contagious.
      • Without treatment, half of children’s warts clear within a year and two-thirds within two years.
      • Salicylic acid from a pharmacy works if you use it every day for up to 3 months. A clinic can also freeze the wart (cryotherapy).
      • Don’t treat warts on your face or genitals yourself. If you have diabetes or poor circulation, ask a doctor before using any wart treatment.
      • See a doctor if a growth bleeds, changes or hurts, or if you aren’t sure it is a wart.

      Types of warts

      TypeWhereWhat it looks like
      Common wartFingers, hands, knees, elbowsA firm, rough, raised bump, often with tiny black dots (clotted blood vessels)
      Plantar wart (verruca)Soles of the feetA flat, hard patch that walking pushes into the skin. Often painful, with black dots
      Flat (plane) wartFace, backs of hands, legsSmall, smooth and slightly raised, often in groups. Shaving can spread them
      Filiform wartFace, around the mouth and noseA thin growth shaped like a finger
      Periungual wartAround the fingernails and toenailsRough growths that can disturb how the nail grows
      Genital wartsGenitals and anusSmall, soft lumps, caused by different HPV types and mostly passed on through sex

      People sometimes call a plantar wart mata ikan because of its black dots, and it is easy to mix up with a corn. Corns and calluses are thickened skin caused by pressure or rubbing, often from tight shoes, and no virus is involved. If the hard skin is gently pared down, a wart shows tiny black dots and a corn doesn’t.

      How warts spread

      Warts pass from person to person, and around your own body, in a few ways:

      • by direct skin contact, or through shared things such as towels and shoes, and wet floors around pools and showers
      • from one part of your body to another, for example when you pick at a wart, bite your nails or shave over warts
      • more easily when the skin is wet, soft or damaged

      After contact, a wart can take weeks or months to appear. Children and teenagers get them most, and so do people with eczema or a weak immune system.

      Genital warts are a sexually transmitted infection, usually caused by HPV types 6 and 11. They spread through skin contact during sex, even when no warts can be seen, but not through towels or toilet seats. They need a doctor. See our guide to STI testing.

      Treating warts at home (ubat ketuat)

      You don’t have to treat a wart. Many go away on their own: half of children’s warts within a year, and two-thirds within two years. If a wart hurts, is spreading or bothers you, a pharmacy has treatments that can help.

      Salicylic acid, sold as a gel, paint or plaster, is the home treatment with the most research behind it. In a Cochrane review it worked better than a dummy treatment. To use it:

      1. Soak the wart in warm water for a few minutes, then dry it.
      2. Gently rub off the dead surface skin with a pumice stone or emery board. Keep that stone or board for the wart only, and don’t share it.
      3. Once a day, put the treatment on the wart and nowhere else, protecting the skin around it.
      4. Carry on for up to 12 weeks. Stopping too early is the most common reason it fails. If the skin gets sore, take a few days off.

      Pharmacy freezing sprays are weaker than freezing at a clinic, and they aren’t suitable for young children.

      Don’t use home treatments on your face, your genitals or broken skin. If you have diabetes, poor circulation or numb feet, get a doctor’s advice first.

      Never cut, burn or dig out a wart yourself. Take care with “wart remover” products sold online: check that the product is registered with the National Pharmaceutical Regulatory Agency (NPRA), and stop using it if it causes pain, bleeding or blisters.

      Clinic treatments

      TreatmentWhat it involves
      Cryotherapy (freezing)The doctor freezes the wart with liquid nitrogen. It stings and usually blisters, and the wart falls off within a week or so. You often need several sessions, 2 to 4 weeks apart. It isn’t usually used for young children
      Curettage and cauteryThe wart is scraped off under local anaesthetic and the base is sealed with heat. It can leave a scar
      LaserUsed for stubborn warts that other treatments haven’t cleared
      Prescription creams and immunotherapyMedicines that irritate the wart or prompt the immune system to clear it, mainly for stubborn or genital warts

      Freezing plus salicylic acid may work better than salicylic acid on its own. Any treatment can leave small marks or pale or dark patches, and warts can come back. Freezing close to a nail can damage it.

      A doctor treats genital warts with prescription creams, freezing or other procedures. Pharmacy wart products shouldn’t be used on them.

      When to see a doctor

      See a doctor if:

      • the growth bleeds, changes in size, shape or colour, itches or hurts
      • you aren’t sure it is a wart, since some skin cancers and other growths, such as seborrhoeic keratoses, can look similar
      • it is on your face or genitals, or you have a lot of warts
      • it keeps coming back or spreading, or 12 weeks of home treatment hasn’t worked
      • you have diabetes, poor circulation or a weak immune system

      Preventing warts

      • Don’t pick, scratch or bite warts, and don’t shave over them.
      • Don’t share towels, shoes or socks.
      • Wear flip-flops in shared showers and around pools, and cover a verruca with a waterproof plaster when you swim.
      • Keep your feet dry and change your socks every day.
      • Vaccines that cover HPV types 6 and 11 protect against most genital warts, and the HPV vaccine also prevents cervical and other cancers. Ask your doctor which vaccine you are being offered, and see our guide to the HPV vaccine.

      Where to get warts treated

      Many GP clinics can confirm that a growth is a wart, freeze or remove it, and refer you to a dermatologist if it is stubborn or unusual. Our directory lists GP clinics across Kuala Lumpur, Selangor and other states. Call ahead to check that the clinic offers cryotherapy.

      Frequently asked questions

      What is ketuat in English?

      Ketuat means warts, the small, rough skin growths caused by the human papillomavirus (HPV). A wart on the sole of the foot is called a verruca or plantar wart.

      What causes ketuat?

      The human papillomavirus (HPV), which gets into the skin through small cuts or breaks.

      Do warts go away on their own?

      Often, yes. Half of children’s warts disappear within a year and two-thirds within two years. In adults they can take longer.

      What is the best treatment for warts?

      The home treatment with the most evidence is salicylic acid, used every day for up to 12 weeks. A clinic can freeze the wart instead, and the two can be used together.

      Are warts contagious?

      Mildly. They spread by touch and on wet surfaces such as shower floors, and you can move them around your own body by picking or shaving.

      What is the difference between a corn and a plantar wart?

      A corn is skin thickened by pressure. A plantar wart is caused by a virus, and it shows tiny black dots when the hard skin is pared down.

      Can I remove a wart myself?

      You can treat common warts and verrucas with pharmacy products. Never cut or burn a wart, and leave warts on the face or genitals to a doctor.

      Does freezing warts hurt?

      It stings while it is done and usually blisters afterwards. You may need several sessions.

      Are genital warts the same as ordinary warts?

      No. Different HPV types cause them, they are usually sexually transmitted, and a doctor needs to treat them.
