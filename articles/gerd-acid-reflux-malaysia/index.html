      GERD and acid reflux (pedih ulu hati): symptoms, triggers, medicines and when it’s more serious

      Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GERD) happens when stomach acid flows back up into the food pipe (oesophagus), causing heartburn (pedih ulu hati) and a sour taste. It is becoming more common in Malaysia and Southeast Asia, often alongside rising weight. Most people can control it with lifestyle changes and medicine. But “gastric” symptoms aren’t always reflux, and some need checking.

      Key points
      • Typical GERD symptoms are a burning feeling in the middle of the chest (heartburn) and acid or food coming back up, often after meals or when lying down. Some people have a cough, hoarse voice or sore throat instead.
      • Chest pain that feels tight or heavy, or spreads to the arm, neck or jaw, could be a heart attack. Call 999.
      • Losing weight if overweight, not eating 3 hours before bed, raising the head of the bed and stopping smoking all help.
      • Antacids give quick relief. Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs), taken 30 to 60 minutes before a meal, are the main treatment for frequent symptoms.
      • See a doctor for difficulty swallowing, weight loss, vomiting blood, black stools, anaemia, or new symptoms after middle age, which may need an endoscopy.

      What is GERD?

      A ring of muscle at the bottom of the food pipe normally stops stomach contents flowing back up. In GERD, acid escapes upwards often enough to cause troublesome symptoms or damage. Occasional heartburn after a big meal is common and isn’t GERD.

      Symptoms:

      • heartburn (pedih ulu hati): a burning feeling in the middle of the chest
      • acid or food coming back up into the throat or mouth (regurgitation), and a sour or bitter taste
      • symptoms that are worse after eating, when lying down or bending over
      • a long-lasting cough, hoarse voice, sore throat, hiccups or bad breath
      • bloating and nausea

      GERD vs “gastric”: in Malaysia, “gastric” is often used for any upper tummy discomfort. Doctors call this dyspepsia, which can come from reflux, stomach ulcers, H. pylori infection, medicines, or occasionally something more serious. See our guide to stomach pain and “gastric”.

      In Southeast Asia, a 2021 regional consensus found GERD is becoming more common, but is mostly mild, without visible damage to the food pipe.

      Heartburn or heart attack?

      A heart attack can feel like indigestion or a burning in the chest. Call 999 if chest pain:

      • feels tight, heavy or squeezing
      • spreads to the arm, neck, jaw, back or stomach
      • comes with breathlessness, sweating, nausea or feeling faint
      • doesn’t settle quickly, or comes on with exertion

      Don’t assume it is “gastric”, especially if you have diabetes, high blood pressure, smoke or are older. See our guide to the warning signs of a heart attack.

      Lifestyle changes that help

      • Lose weight if you are overweight. This has the strongest evidence. More than half of Malaysian adults are overweight or obese: see our BMI calculator.
      • Don’t eat for 3 hours before bed, and have smaller meals rather than big ones, especially late at night (including supper).
      • Raise the head of your bed by 10 to 20 cm with blocks under the legs, rather than piling up pillows, if symptoms are worse at night.
      • Sleeping on your left side may reduce night-time reflux, though the evidence is limited.
      • Stop smoking and cut down on alcohol.
      • Notice your own triggers. Common ones include coffee, alcohol, chocolate, tomatoes, fatty and spicy food, but you don’t need to cut out foods that don’t bother you.
      • Avoid tight belts and waistbands.

      Medicines for reflux (ubat gastrik)

      H2 blockers (such as famotidine)Reduce acid productionAn alternative for milder or night-time symptoms

      Taking PPIs properly:

      • Take them 30 to 60 minutes before breakfast (or before your evening meal if advised). They work best on an empty stomach before food.
      • They take a few days to work fully.
      • After a course, your doctor may try stepping down to the lowest dose that works, or stopping. When you stop, acid symptoms can briefly return (rebound) for a week or two, which doesn’t mean you still need them.
      • Long-term PPIs are safe for most people who need them. Some studies have linked long-term use to low vitamin B12 and weaker bones, but don’t stop them on your own if a doctor has prescribed them for a reason. Review them with your doctor at least yearly.
      • Don’t use pharmacy PPIs for more than 2 weeks without seeing a doctor.

      When you need a scope (endoscopy)

      See a doctor soon if you have reflux or “gastric” symptoms with:

      • difficulty or pain when swallowing, or food sticking
      • weight loss without trying
      • vomiting blood or what looks like coffee grounds, or black, tarry stools (go to an emergency department)
      • anaemia, or feeling very tired and breathless
      • persistent vomiting
      • new symptoms starting in middle age, or a family history of stomach cancer
      • symptoms that don’t improve with treatment

      The doctor may refer you for an endoscopy (a thin camera passed through the mouth into the stomach). Stomach cancer is more common in Asia than in the West and can occur at a younger age, so Asian experts suggest a lower age threshold for checking new symptoms. Barrett’s oesophagus, a change in the lining of the food pipe caused by long-term reflux, is rare in Southeast Asia.

      H. pylori

      Helicobacter pylori is a bacterium that infects the stomach lining. It causes most stomach ulcers and is a major risk factor for stomach cancer. Clearing it largely stops ulcers from coming back.

      • In Malaysia, studies have found infection rates of about 10 to 25% in Malays, 35 to 55% in Chinese, 50 to 60% in Indians, and higher in some indigenous communities in Sabah and Sarawak. Rates have fallen over time.
      • It is tested with a breath test or stool test, or a biopsy during endoscopy.
      • Stop PPIs for at least 2 weeks and antibiotics for 4 weeks before a breath or stool test, or the result may be falsely negative. Ask your doctor before stopping.
      • Treatment is a 1 to 2 week course of two antibiotics plus a PPI, followed by a test to confirm it has gone.

      Reflux in pregnancy

      Heartburn is common in pregnancy because of hormones and the growing baby pressing on the stomach. Eat small meals, avoid eating within 3 hours of bed, and raise your head and shoulders at night. Antacids or alginates are often suitable, but check with a pharmacist or doctor first, and take them at least 2 hours apart from iron or folic acid tablets. See our pregnancy week-by-week guide.

      Where to get reflux checked

      A GP can confirm reflux, prescribe and review acid-reducing medicine, test for H. pylori and refer you for an endoscopy if needed. Our directory lists GP clinics across Kuala Lumpur, Selangor and other states, including clinics open late.

      Frequently asked questions

      What is GERD?

      Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease: stomach acid flowing back into the food pipe often enough to cause heartburn, regurgitation or other symptoms.

      What does pedih ulu hati mean?

      It is heartburn: a burning feeling in the middle of the chest or upper tummy, often caused by acid reflux.

      How can I tell heartburn from a heart attack?

      You can’t always tell. Chest pain that is tight or heavy, spreads to the arm, neck or jaw, or comes with breathlessness or sweating needs a 999 call.

      What is the best medicine for GERD?

      Antacids give quick relief for occasional symptoms. For frequent symptoms, a PPI taken 30 to 60 minutes before a meal for 4 to 8 weeks is the main treatment.

      Can GERD be cured?

      Many people control it well with weight loss, meal timing and medicine, and some can stop medicine after a course. It can come back.

      Is it safe to take PPIs long term?

      For most people who need them, yes. Have your doctor review them regularly and use the lowest dose that works.

      How is H. pylori tested?

      With a breath test, stool test or biopsy during endoscopy. Stop PPIs for 2 weeks before a breath or stool test, after checking with your doctor.

      When does gastric pain need a scope?

      With difficulty swallowing, weight loss, vomiting blood, black stools, anaemia, new symptoms in middle age, a family history of stomach cancer, or symptoms that don’t improve.
