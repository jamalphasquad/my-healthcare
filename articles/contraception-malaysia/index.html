      Contraception in Malaysia: the pill (pil perancang), implant, IUD, injection and the morning-after pill

      There are many safe, effective ways to prevent pregnancy, and the best one is the one that suits your health, your plans and your life. In Malaysia, about 1 in 4 women who want to avoid pregnancy aren’t using an effective method. Here is how the options compare, and where to get them.

      Key points
      • The implant and the coil (IUD) are the most effective methods, at more than 99% in everyday use, because there is nothing to remember.
      • The pill is more than 99% effective if taken perfectly, but about 91% in everyday use, because pills get missed.
      • Only condoms protect against sexually transmitted infections, including HIV.
      • The morning-after pill works best taken as soon as possible after unprotected sex, within 3 to 5 days depending on the type. In Malaysia, a pharmacist can supply levonorgestrel emergency pills without a doctor’s prescription.
      • LPPKN’s Klinik Nur Sejahtera offer counselling, pills, injections, implants and coils at low fees, as do Klinik Kesihatan and private clinics.

      How well each method works

      MethodEveryday useHow long it lasts
      ImplantOver 99%3 years
      Hormonal coil (IUS)Over 99%3 to 8 years, depending on type
      Copper coil (IUD)Over 99%5 to 10 years, depending on type

      “Everyday use” figures, from the NHS and US CDC, allow for real-life mistakes such as missed pills. With perfect use, the pill, injection and condoms all work much better.

      In Malaysia: the national Population and Family Survey found that in 2014 only about a third of married women aged 15 to 49 used a modern method. LPPKN figures for 2022 suggest about 1 in 4 women who want to avoid or delay pregnancy have an unmet need for contraception.

      Long-acting methods: implant, coil and injection

      Implant

      • A small, flexible rod about the size of a matchstick, placed under the skin of the upper arm through a tiny cut after a local anaesthetic. It takes a few minutes.
      • Releases a progestogen hormone and is licensed for 3 years. It can be removed at any time, and fertility returns quickly.
      • The most common side effect is changes to periods: they may become lighter, stop, or become irregular or longer. There isn’t good evidence that it causes weight gain.
      • A 2023 study of Ministry of Health clinics found the implant was the most cost-effective method they offered.

      Coil (IUD and IUS)

      • A small T-shaped device placed in the womb by a trained doctor or nurse.
      • The copper coil has no hormones, works straight away, lasts 5 to 10 years, and can also be used as emergency contraception. Periods may become heavier or more painful at first.
      • The hormonal coil lasts 3 to 8 years and usually makes periods lighter and less painful. See our guide to period pain.
      • Fertility returns as soon as it is removed.

      Injection

      • A progestogen injection every 8 to 13 weeks, usually every 3 months.
      • Periods often stop. Some people gain weight, and it can thin the bones slightly with long use, which recovers after stopping.
      • It can take up to a year for fertility to return after stopping, so it may not suit you if you plan to get pregnant soon.

      The pill (pil perancang)

      The combined pill contains oestrogen and progestogen. It is taken daily, usually for 21 days followed by a 7-day break, or every day with dummy pills. If you start it on day 1 to 5 of your period, it protects you straight away. Otherwise use condoms for the first 7 days.

      • Benefits: periods are often lighter, more regular and less painful, and it can help acne. It lowers the risk of womb, ovarian and bowel cancer.
      • Side effects: spotting, headaches, nausea, sore breasts and mood changes, which often settle within a few months. There is no good evidence that it causes weight gain.
      • Risks: a small increased risk of blood clots, and a slight increase in breast and cervical cancer risk while taking it, which returns to normal 10 years after stopping.
      • It may not suit you if you smoke and are 35 or over, have migraine with aura, high blood pressure, a history of blood clots, stroke or heart disease, are very overweight, or have had breast cancer. A doctor will check your health and blood pressure before prescribing it.

      Missed pills: if you are less than 48 hours late with one combined pill, take it as soon as you remember and carry on. If you have missed two or more, follow the leaflet in your pack, use condoms for 7 days, and ask a pharmacist whether you need emergency contraception.

      The progestogen-only pill (“mini-pill”) has no oestrogen, so it suits more people, including those who are breastfeeding, smoke or can’t take oestrogen. It is taken every day without a break, and needs to be taken at the same time each day.

      For women with PCOS, the pill can also help with irregular periods, acne and excess hair: see our guide to PCOS (PMOS).

      Condoms

      Condoms are the only method that also protects against sexually transmitted infections, including HIV, syphilis and gonorrhoea. Many people use them alongside another method for extra protection. Use a new one every time, check the expiry date, and use water-based lubricant with latex condoms. See our guides to STI testing and HIV and PrEP.

      The morning-after pill and emergency contraception

      If you have had sex without contraception, or your method failed (for example, a split condom or missed pills), emergency contraception can prevent pregnancy:

      OptionTake or fit withinNotes
      Levonorgestrel pill72 hours (3 days)Works best the sooner it is taken. May work less well in people weighing more than about 70 kg
      Ulipristal pill120 hours (5 days)More effective than levonorgestrel later in the 5 days
      Copper coil5 daysThe most effective option, over 99%, and then gives ongoing contraception
      • Emergency pills work by delaying ovulation. They don’t cause an abortion and won’t harm an existing pregnancy.
      • In Malaysia, levonorgestrel emergency pills are classed so that a pharmacist can supply them without a doctor’s prescription, after a short consultation. Ask the pharmacist about other options, which may need a doctor.
      • If you vomit within a few hours of taking the pill, ask a pharmacist or doctor whether to take another.
      • Do a pregnancy test if your next period is more than 7 days late or unusually light.
      • Emergency contraception isn’t meant for regular use. Talk to a doctor or LPPKN clinic about an ongoing method.

      Common myths

      • “Contraception causes infertility.” No. Fertility returns soon after stopping the pill, implant or coil. After the injection it can take several months, up to a year.
      • “The pill makes you fat.” Studies haven’t shown that the pill causes significant weight gain. The injection is the method most linked to weight gain.
      • “You don’t need contraception after 40.” You can still get pregnant until menopause, so contraception is still needed.
      • “The morning-after pill is an abortion pill.” No. It prevents or delays ovulation and doesn’t end a pregnancy.

      Where to get contraception in Malaysia

      • LPPKN (Klinik Nur Sejahtera): family planning counselling, pills, injections, implants and coils. Fees are low: for example, RM5 registration and RM10 consultation, with pills from RM5, the injection RM36, and implant insertion RM500, according to LPPKN’s fee list updated in June 2026. LPPKN also gives free contraceptives to eligible B40 women.
      • Klinik Kesihatan: government health clinics offer the pill, injection, coil and implant.
      • Private GP and women’s clinics: you can usually be seen quickly, including for implant and coil fitting. Call ahead to check which methods they offer.
      • Pharmacies: condoms, and emergency contraception after a consultation with the pharmacist. Ongoing pills need a doctor’s assessment first.
      • FRHAM (Federation of Reproductive Health Associations, Malaysia) state associations also provide contraception and sexual health services.

      Unmarried women: doctors and health researchers say the Ministry of Health has no policy against providing contraception to unmarried women, but some women have reported being turned away or asked for a marriage certificate. Practice varies between clinics, so it may be worth calling ahead.

      Where to get contraception advice

      A GP can help you choose a method, check your blood pressure and health, prescribe the pill or injection, and fit or refer you for an implant or coil. Our directory lists GP clinics across Kuala Lumpur, Selangor and other states, including clinics open late and at weekends.

      Frequently asked questions

      What is the most effective contraception?

      The implant and the coil (hormonal or copper) are more than 99% effective in everyday use, because there is nothing to remember.

      How effective is the pill?

      More than 99% if taken perfectly, but about 91% in everyday use, because pills are sometimes missed or taken late.

      Can I buy the morning-after pill without a prescription in Malaysia?

      Yes, levonorgestrel emergency pills can be supplied by a pharmacist without a doctor’s prescription, after a short consultation.

      How long after sex can I take the morning-after pill?

      Levonorgestrel within 72 hours and ulipristal within 120 hours, but the sooner the better. A copper coil fitted within 5 days is the most effective option.

      How long does the implant last?

      It is licensed for 3 years, and can be removed at any time if you want to get pregnant or switch methods.

      Does contraception cause infertility?

      No. Fertility returns soon after stopping most methods. After the injection it can take several months, up to a year.

      Where can I get contraception in Malaysia?

      At LPPKN’s Klinik Nur Sejahtera, Klinik Kesihatan, private GP and women’s clinics, and, for condoms and emergency pills, pharmacies.

      Does the pill protect against STIs?

      No. Only condoms protect against sexually transmitted infections, including HIV.
