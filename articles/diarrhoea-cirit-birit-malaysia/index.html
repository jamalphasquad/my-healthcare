      Cirit birit (diarrhoea): causes, treatment, which medicines work and when to see a doctor

      Cirit birit is the Malay term for diarrhoea: passing loose or watery stools three or more times a day. Most cases clear up by themselves within 5 to 7 days. The main danger is dehydration, so replacing fluids and salts comes first. Here is what works, what to avoid, and when to see a doctor.

      Key points
      • Diarrhoea means 3 or more loose stools a day. It usually clears up in 5 to 7 days. Diarrhoea that lasts more than 4 weeks is chronic and needs checking.
      • Oral rehydration salts (ORS) are the most important treatment. Follow the instructions on the packet. Plain water, soft drinks, apple juice and young coconut water aren’t good replacements for ORS in children.
      • Adults can use loperamide for short-term relief, but not if there is blood in the stool or a high fever. It is not recommended for children with gastroenteritis.
      • Antibiotics usually don’t help, as most cases are caused by viruses. Kaolin and charcoal have no proven benefit.
      • See a doctor for blood in the stool, diarrhoea lasting more than 7 days, signs of dehydration, or if you can’t keep fluids down. Babies need help sooner.

      What is cirit birit?

      Cirit birit (also written cirit-birit) means diarrhoea. The World Health Organization defines it as passing 3 or more loose or watery stools a day, or more often than is normal for you. Soft, pasty stools in breastfed babies are normal.

      TypeHow long it lasts
      AcuteUsually less than 7 days, and up to 14 days
      Persistent14 days or more
      ChronicMore than 4 weeks

      In Malaysia, a national survey found that about 4 in 100 children under 5 had diarrhoea in the previous two weeks, with higher rates in homes using untreated water.

      Common causes

      • Infections: viruses such as rotavirus and norovirus, bacteria and parasites. See our guide to food poisoning and gastroenteritis.
      • Medicines: antibiotics, metformin, gliptins, magnesium products, anti-inflammatory painkillers and some blood pressure tablets (ACE inhibitors)
      • Food and drink: lactose intolerance, artificial sweeteners and alcohol
      • Bowel conditions: irritable bowel syndrome, coeliac disease, inflammatory bowel disease and bile acid diarrhoea
      • Other conditions: an overactive thyroid
      • Bowel cancer: loose stools or more frequent stools that continue, especially with blood or weight loss, should be checked

      Oral rehydration salts: the most important treatment

      Diarrhoea drains water and salts from the body. Oral rehydration salts (ORS, or garam rehidrasi oral) replace both, using the right balance of salt and sugar to help your gut absorb water.

      • Mix it as the packet says. Sachet sizes differ. The Malaysian Ministry of Health’s training manual uses one sachet in 250ml of cooled boiled water for its product, while WHO-style packets are made up with about 1 litre.
      • Throw away any mixture after 24 hours.
      • Adults: drink as much as you want, in small frequent sips, especially after each loose stool.
      • Children without dehydration: give an extra 50 to 100ml after each loose stool up to age 2, and 100 to 200ml from age 2.
      • If vomiting: wait 5 to 10 minutes, then give it more slowly. For a child under 2, give a teaspoon every 1 to 2 minutes.

      What about other drinks? The 2026 Malaysian children’s guideline lists cola, apple juice, young coconut water, chicken broth, tea and isotonic sports drinks as unsuitable for treating dehydration. They have much less sodium than ORS, and very sugary drinks can make diarrhoea worse. Healthy adults with mild diarrhoea can usually manage with fluids plus salty food, but ORS is the better choice if you are older, unwell or passing a lot of stool.

      Ubat cirit birit: which medicines work?

      MedicineDoes it help?
      LoperamideReduces stool frequency in adults. Don’t use with blood in the stool or a high fever.
      Bismuth subsalicylateCan help adults. Not for under-12s or people with aspirin allergy, gout, kidney problems or on blood thinners. Turns the tongue and stools black.
      ProbioticsA 2020 Cochrane review of 82 studies found probably little or no difference to diarrhoea lasting 48 hours or more. The Malaysian children’s guideline allows selected strains as an add-on.
      Zinc (children)WHO recommends 10 to 14 days of zinc. The 2026 Malaysian guideline suggests it mainly for malnourished children or those likely to be deficient. Ask your doctor.
      Racecadotril and diosmectite (children)May be considered by a doctor. Evidence is limited.
      Kaolin and charcoalMinistry of Health training material says these have no benefit. The Malaysian children’s guideline doesn’t recommend kaolin/pectin.
      AntibioticsUsually not needed. Doctors use them for specific infections such as cholera, Shigella dysentery and some serious infections.

      Loperamide for adults: UK guidance is 2 capsules at first, then 1 after each loose stool, with a maximum of 6 capsules in 24 hours when bought without a prescription, for no more than 48 hours. Taking too much can cause dangerous heart rhythm problems. It is available from pharmacists for ages 12 and over under UK rules, but the Malaysian children’s guideline advises against it in children with gastroenteritis, because deaths have been reported, especially under the age of 3. Ask a pharmacist or doctor if you are pregnant or have liver disease.

      Antibiotics can harm. With certain E. coli infections, they can raise the risk of serious kidney complications. Don’t use leftover antibiotics.

      What to eat

      • Keep eating as soon as you feel able to. Children shouldn’t go without food for more than 4 to 6 hours after rehydration has started.
      • A strict “BRAT” diet (bananas, rice, apple sauce and toast) is no longer needed and is unnecessarily restrictive, though plain foods such as rice porridge may be easier to manage.
      • Avoid fatty and spicy food, fizzy drinks, undiluted fruit juice, caffeine and alcohol while you recover.
      • Babies: continue breastfeeding. Don’t dilute formula, and there is usually no need to switch to lactose-free milk.

      When to see a doctor

      Signs of dehydration: thirst, dark urine, passing little urine, dizziness, a dry mouth and sunken eyes. In babies, also look for a sunken soft spot on the head, few tears, fewer wet nappies, and drowsiness or irritability.

      Adults should see a doctor promptly for:

      • blood in the stool
      • diarrhoea lasting more than 7 days, or vomiting for more than 2 days
      • being unable to keep fluids down
      • signs of dehydration that continue despite ORS
      • diarrhoea with a high fever, or after recent antibiotics, if it is severe
      • older age or a weak immune system

      Children and babies should be seen urgently if:

      • they can’t drink or breastfeed, or are getting sicker
      • there is blood in the stool or a fever
      • they keep vomiting, particularly if the vomit is green
      • they are unusually sleepy, or show signs of dehydration
      • you are worried about a baby under 12 months, or the baby has stopped feeding

      Go to an emergency department or call 999 for: vomiting blood, severe tummy pain, confusion, a stiff neck, pale, blue or grey skin, severe breathlessness, or a seizure.

      In dengue season, diarrhoea or vomiting more than 3 times in 24 hours during a fever can be a warning sign, and needs to be checked.

      Diabetes, kidney and heart medicines

      If you can’t eat and drink normally, some medicines can become risky, including SGLT2 inhibitors, metformin, sulfonylureas, GLP-1 medicines, water tablets, ACE inhibitors, ARBs and anti-inflammatory painkillers. UK “sick day rules” advise pausing these until you are well. Never stop insulin.

      We didn’t find equivalent Malaysian guidance, so ask your doctor or pharmacist for a plan in advance. Seek help the same day if you take an SGLT2 inhibitor and feel unwell, as a dangerous complication (ketoacidosis) can occur even with normal blood sugar. See our guide to blood sugar levels.

      Travel, and when stool tests are needed

      Most traveller’s diarrhoea is caused by bacteria. Treat it with fluids, and carry ORS if you are travelling. If it lasts more than 2 weeks, a parasite may be the cause. Going on pilgrimage? See our guide to Umrah and Haj health.

      Your doctor may arrange a stool test if there is blood in the stool, severe illness, diarrhoea lasting more than 7 days, a weak immune system, or an outbreak. Chronic diarrhoea may need further tests, such as blood tests or a colonoscopy.

      Frequently asked questions

      What is cirit birit in English?

      Cirit birit means diarrhoea (spelled diarrhea in American English): passing loose or watery stools 3 or more times a day.

      What is the best ubat cirit birit?

      Oral rehydration salts are the most important treatment. Adults can also use loperamide for short-term relief if there is no blood in the stool or high fever. Ask a pharmacist.

      How do I stop diarrhoea quickly?

      Replace fluids with ORS and eat as normal when you can. Loperamide can reduce stool frequency in adults. Most diarrhoea clears up within 5 to 7 days.

      Is coconut water good for diarrhoea?

      Not as a replacement for ORS. Malaysian children’s guidelines list young coconut water as unsuitable, as it has much less sodium than ORS.

      Do I need antibiotics for diarrhoea?

      Usually not, as most cases are viral. Antibiotics are used for specific infections, and can sometimes be harmful.

      How long is too long for diarrhoea?

      See a doctor if it lasts more than 7 days in an adult, or sooner with blood, dehydration or a fever. Diarrhoea lasting more than 4 weeks is chronic.

      Can I give loperamide to my child?

      It is not recommended for children with gastroenteritis, as deaths have been reported, especially under the age of 3. See a doctor.

      Should I stop breastfeeding if my baby has diarrhoea?

      No. Keep breastfeeding, and give extra fluids as your doctor advises.
