      Frozen shoulder and shoulder pain (sakit bahu): stages, what helps, and when to see a doctor

      Frozen shoulder (adhesive capsulitis) happens when the capsule around the shoulder joint becomes inflamed, tight and thick, causing pain and stiffness that can last a year or more. It mostly affects people aged 40 to 60, especially women and people with diabetes. Staying gently active helps, and treatment can ease the pain while the shoulder recovers.

      Key points
      • Frozen shoulder affects about 2 to 5% of people, mostly aged 40 to 60, and more often women. Diabetes makes it about 5 times more likely.
      • It causes pain, often worse at night, and stiffness in every direction, so it becomes hard to reach behind your back or put on a shirt.
      • It usually goes through a painful freezing stage, a stiff frozen stage and a slow thawing stage, over 1 to 3 years. Some people keep a little stiffness.
      • Keep the shoulder moving. Gentle exercises, painkillers, physiotherapy and a steroid injection in the early painful stage all help.
      • In a large UK trial, surgery and manipulation weren’t clearly better than physiotherapy with a steroid injection, so they are usually kept for shoulders that don’t improve.

      What is frozen shoulder, and who gets it?

      The shoulder joint is surrounded by a capsule of tough tissue. In frozen shoulder, the capsule becomes inflamed, then thickens and tightens, leaving less room for the joint to move. The cause is often unclear.

      It is more likely if you:

      • are aged 40 to 60, especially if you are a woman
      • have diabetes. About 1 in 8 people with diabetes get frozen shoulder, about 5 times the rate in other people, and it can be more stubborn. In Malaysia, 15.6% of adults have diabetes, and 2 in 5 of them don’t know it. If you have frozen shoulder, it is worth checking your blood sugar.
      • have thyroid disease, heart disease or Parkinson’s disease
      • have had your arm kept still after an injury, fracture or surgery, even a minor wrist or elbow injury in a sling or cast

      Between 2 and 5% of people develop frozen shoulder, and in up to 3 in 10 it later affects the other shoulder.

      Symptoms and stages

      Frozen shoulder causes a dull ache around the outer shoulder and upper arm, pain at night that disturbs sleep, and stiffness when moving the arm in any direction, even when someone else moves it for you. Everyday tasks such as fastening a bra, reaching behind your back or into a high cupboard become hard.

      StageUsually lastsWhat happens
      Freezing2 to 9 monthsPain gradually builds, and movement starts to get worse
      Frozen4 to 12 monthsPain may ease, but the shoulder is very stiff
      Thawing6 months to 2 yearsMovement slowly returns

      The whole process can take up to 3 years. These stages overlap, and recovery isn’t always complete: studies suggest many people regain most but not all of their movement, and people with diabetes are more likely to keep some stiffness.

      Diagnosis is usually made by examining the shoulder. An X-ray is often normal and is used to rule out other problems such as arthritis. An MRI isn’t usually needed. A doctor may do blood tests to check for diabetes or thyroid disease.

      What helps

      • Keep moving as much as you can, within your pain. Using a sling or resting the arm for long periods makes stiffness worse.
      • Painkillers: paracetamol, then an anti-inflammatory such as ibuprofen if suitable.
      • Heat: a heat pack for up to 20 minutes before exercises can help.
      • Physiotherapy: usually for at least 6 weeks, with exercises to do at home.
      • Steroid injection: an injection into the shoulder joint can ease pain and help movement, especially early on, in the first few months. Adding it to physiotherapy works better than physiotherapy alone. In people with diabetes, it can raise blood sugar for a few days, so check your levels.

      Two simple exercises (stop if the pain gets worse):

      • Pendulum: hold a table with your good arm, bend forward and let the sore arm hang. Swing it gently back and forth, then in small circles, for 2 to 3 minutes, several times a day.
      • Wall climb: face a wall and walk your fingers up it as high as you can without too much pain. Hold for 15 to 30 seconds, and repeat 2 to 4 times.

      If it doesn’t improve: options include a large-volume injection to stretch the joint (hydrodilatation), which helps mainly in the short term, manipulation under anaesthetic, and keyhole surgery to release the tight capsule. In the UK FROST trial of 503 people, published in The Lancet in 2020, surgery, manipulation and physiotherapy with a steroid injection all gave similar results at a year, and surgery had more serious complications. So these are usually kept for shoulders that remain painful and stiff despite other treatment.

      Be careful with forceful “cracking” or deep massage. Malaysian specialists warn that forcing a stiff shoulder can cause fractures in weak bones or tear the tendons. See a doctor first.

      Other causes of shoulder pain (sakit bahu)

      CauseTypical signs
      Rotator cuff problemsPain when lifting the arm out to the side or overhead, weakness, night pain. Movement is usually fine if someone else moves your arm. Most improve with exercises over weeks to months, without surgery
      Calcific tendinopathySudden, severe shoulder pain from calcium deposits in a tendon
      ArthritisPain with activity, grinding, stiffness, usually in older adults. Shows on X-ray
      A problem in the neckPain spreading from the neck into the shoulder and arm, sometimes with tingling. See our guide to slipped discs

      Call 999 if arm or shoulder pain comes with chest pain or tightness, breathlessness or sweating, as it could be a heart attack. Don’t drive yourself.

      Go to an emergency department if you can’t move your arm after an injury, the shoulder has changed shape, or the arm is numb, hot or cold. Don’t try to put a dislocated shoulder back yourself.

      When to see a doctor

      • shoulder pain that is getting worse, or hasn’t improved after 2 weeks
      • you can’t move your arm normally, or pain wakes you at night
      • both shoulders are affected, or you have a fever
      • you have diabetes and a stiff shoulder

      After an injury or surgery, ask when you can start gentle shoulder exercises, to help prevent stiffness.

      Where to get a shoulder checked

      A GP can examine your shoulder, arrange an X-ray and blood sugar test, prescribe pain relief, and refer you for physiotherapy, an injection or to an orthopaedic specialist.       Frequently asked questions

      What are the first signs of frozen shoulder?

      Gradually worsening shoulder pain, often at night, followed by stiffness that makes it hard to reach behind your back or overhead.

      How long does frozen shoulder last?

      Usually 1 to 3 years, through freezing, frozen and thawing stages. Treatment can ease pain during this time.

      Who gets frozen shoulder?

      Mostly people aged 40 to 60, more often women. People with diabetes or thyroid disease, or whose arm has been kept still after an injury, are at higher risk.

      Does frozen shoulder go away on its own?

      It usually improves slowly over time, but some people keep some stiffness. Exercises, physiotherapy and injections can help.

      Should I rest a frozen shoulder?

      No. Keep moving it gently, within your pain. Long periods of rest make stiffness worse.

      Does a steroid injection help frozen shoulder?

      Yes, particularly in the early, painful stage. It works better alongside physiotherapy.

      Do I need surgery for frozen shoulder?

      Most people don’t. A large trial found surgery wasn’t clearly better than physiotherapy with a steroid injection, so it is kept for shoulders that don’t improve.

      Can shoulder pain be a heart attack?

      Yes. Call 999 if arm or shoulder pain comes with chest pain or tightness, breathlessness or sweating.
