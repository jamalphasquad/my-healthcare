      URTI and the common cold (selsema): symptoms, what helps, antibiotics, and when to see a doctor

      URTI (upper respiratory tract infection) is the medical name for infections of the nose, sinuses and throat, most often the common cold (selsema). It is one of the most common diagnoses in Malaysian clinics, and it is almost always caused by viruses. That means antibiotics don’t help, yet about half of URTI patients in a national study were given them. Here is what does help.

      Key points
      • URTI covers colds, sore throats, sinusitis and laryngitis. More than 200 viruses cause colds, most often rhinovirus.
      • Cold symptoms build over 2 to 3 days and usually clear within 1 to 2 weeks. A cough can last 3 to 4 weeks.
      • Rest, fluids, paracetamol or ibuprofen, saline nose drops and honey for coughs (not under 1 year) help. Zinc and vitamin C have only small or uncertain effects.
      • Antibiotics don’t help colds and cause side effects in about 1 in 10 people. In a national study of Malaysian clinics, 46% of URTI patients were still given them.
      • See a doctor for breathing difficulty, fever lasting more than 3 days, symptoms that get better then worse, or a baby under 3 months with a temperature of 38°C or more.

      What is URTI?

      Doctors write URTI on MCs and notes to mean an infection of the upper airways: the nose, sinuses, throat and voice box. It includes:

      • the common cold (selsema)
      • sore throat and tonsillitis
      • sinusitis
      • laryngitis (a hoarse or lost voice)

      More than 200 viruses cause colds, most often rhinovirus, as well as common coronaviruses, adenovirus and others. In a 2014 national survey of Malaysian primary care clinics, URTI was the most common diagnosis among patients given antibiotics, accounting for about half of all antibiotic prescriptions.

      Symptoms and how long they last

      • a blocked or runny nose (hidung tersumbat), sneezing
      • sore or scratchy throat
      • cough
      • mild fever, aches and tiredness, especially in children
      • hoarse voice, and pressure in the ears or face

      Symptoms build over 2 to 3 days, and most colds clear within 1 to 2 weeks. A cough can carry on for 3 to 4 weeks. Green or yellow mucus is normal in a cold and doesn’t mean you need antibiotics.

      Cold, flu, COVID-19 or dengue?

      • A cold starts gradually and mainly affects the nose and throat. You can usually carry on.
      • Flu comes on within hours, with high fever, aches and exhaustion.
      • COVID-19 can look very similar to a cold or flu. A test is the only way to tell.
      • Dengue usually causes a high fever, headache, pain behind the eyes and body aches, often without a runny nose. If you have a high fever without cold symptoms, see a doctor.

      What helps (ubat selsema)

      • Rest and drink plenty of fluids.
      • Paracetamol or ibuprofen for fever, sore throat and aches. If dengue is possible, use paracetamol only. See our guide to painkillers.
      • Saline (salt-water) nose drops or spray may ease a blocked nose, and are safe for children.
      • Honey, alone or in warm water with lemon, can ease a cough. A Cochrane review found it probably helps children’s cough for up to 3 days. Never give honey to babies under 1.
      • Gargling with warm salt water can soothe a sore throat in adults.
      • Decongestant nasal sprays can give a small improvement in a blocked nose, but don’t use them for more than a week, and not in children under 6.

      Supplements:

      • Vitamin C taken regularly doesn’t stop you catching colds, and may only slightly shorten them. Starting it once you are ill shows no consistent benefit.
      • Zinc may shorten a cold by about 2 days, but the evidence is weak, and it can cause nausea and taste changes.
      • Steam inhalation hasn’t been shown to help, and bowls of hot water can scald children.

      Combination cold remedies: many contain paracetamol, so don’t take them with separate paracetamol, to avoid an overdose. Antihistamines help a little only in the first 1 to 2 days.

      Colds in children

      • Young children often have 6 or more colds a year, especially in nursery or school.
      • Malaysia’s Drug Control Authority says cough and cold medicines containing antihistamines, decongestants or cough suppressants shouldn’t be given to children under 2, and only on a doctor’s or pharmacist’s advice for those aged 2 to 6.
      • Use saline drops, fluids and paracetamol or ibuprofen, dosed by weight. Don’t give aspirin to children under 16.
      • Honey is fine from age 1. Avoid throat lozenges under 4, as they are a choking risk.

      See our guides to fever in children and RSV, which can make babies very unwell.

      Why antibiotics don’t help

      Colds and most URTIs are caused by viruses, and antibiotics only work on bacteria. A 2025 Cochrane review found antibiotics don’t help colds, even with green or yellow mucus, and adults who took them had about 2.6 times the risk of side effects such as diarrhoea, nausea and rash.

      • In a 2014 national survey of Malaysian clinics, 46% of URTI patients were prescribed antibiotics, 58% at private clinics and 17% at public clinics.
      • Unnecessary antibiotics fuel antibiotic resistance, making future infections harder to treat. Malaysia has a national action plan to reduce this.
      • If your doctor isn’t sure, they may suggest a “just in case” prescription to collect only if you aren’t improving after a few days. Studies show this greatly reduces antibiotic use, with similar recovery.

      Antibiotics may be needed for some bacterial throat infections, sinus infections that last more than about 10 days, or complications such as pneumonia. Your doctor will decide.

      When to see a doctor

      See a doctor if:

      • you have a fever lasting more than 3 days
      • you are breathless, wheezy, or have chest pain
      • you get better, then worse again
      • symptoms haven’t improved after 10 days, or a cough lasts more than 3 weeks (see our guide to a cough that won’t go away)
      • you have ear pain, or severe pain in the face or forehead
      • you have a long-term condition such as asthma, diabetes or heart disease, or a weak immune system
      • you are showing signs of dehydration

      Get urgent help for difficulty breathing, coughing up blood, a swollen, painful neck on one side, or a baby under 3 months with a temperature of 38°C or more (3 to 6 months: 39°C or more). See our guide on clinic or emergency department.

      Stopping it spreading

      • You are infectious from a few days before symptoms until they have gone.
      • Wash your hands often, use and bin tissues, and cough into your elbow.
      • Don’t share towels or cups, and stay home if you have a fever.
      • The yearly flu vaccine doesn’t prevent colds, but does protect against flu.

      If you need time off work, see our guide to medical certificates. An MC requires an in-person examination.

      Frequently asked questions

      What does URTI mean?

      Upper respiratory tract infection: an infection of the nose, sinuses, throat or voice box, most often a common cold. It is usually caused by a virus.

      How long does a cold last?

      Most colds clear within 1 to 2 weeks. A cough can last 3 to 4 weeks.

      Do I need antibiotics for URTI?

      Usually not. Most URTIs are viral, and antibiotics don’t help them but can cause side effects and resistance.

      What is the best medicine for a cold (ubat selsema)?

      Rest, fluids, paracetamol or ibuprofen, saline nose drops and honey for a cough. Decongestant sprays can help for a few days.

      Does vitamin C cure a cold?

      No. Taking it regularly doesn’t prevent colds and may only slightly shorten them. Starting it once ill shows no consistent benefit.

      Can I give cough and cold medicine to my child?

      Not under 2, and only on a doctor’s or pharmacist’s advice for ages 2 to 6. Saline drops, fluids and paracetamol are safer choices.

      Is green mucus a sign of infection needing antibiotics?

      No. Green or yellow mucus is common in viral colds and doesn’t mean you need antibiotics.

      When should I see a doctor for a cold?

      For fever over 3 days, breathlessness or chest pain, getting worse after improving, or symptoms lasting more than 10 days.
