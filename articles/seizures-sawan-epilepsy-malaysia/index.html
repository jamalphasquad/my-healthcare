      Sawan (seizures and fits): first aid, causes and epilepsy in Malaysia

      Sawan is the Malay word for a seizure or fit. Seeing one is frightening, but most stop by themselves within a few minutes, and simple first aid helps keep the person safe. Never put anything in their mouth. Here is what to do, when to call for help, and what happens next if it could be epilepsy.

      Key points
      • During a seizure: time it, move dangerous objects away, cushion the head, and stay with the person. Once the jerking stops, turn them on their side.
      • Never put a spoon, your fingers or anything else in their mouth. A person can’t swallow their tongue. Don’t hold them down, and don’t give food, water or pills until they are fully awake.
      • Call 999 if it is a first seizure, it lasts more than 5 minutes, seizures repeat without recovery, the person is injured, or they have trouble breathing.
      • One seizure isn’t the same as epilepsy. Epilepsy generally means two or more unprovoked seizures, or one with a high risk of more.
      • Up to 70% of people with epilepsy can become seizure-free with the right treatment. Don’t stop medicines without specialist advice, especially in pregnancy.

      First aid: what to do if someone has a seizure

      1. Stay calm and time it. Note when the seizure started.
      2. Make the area safe. Move sharp or hot objects away. Only move the person if they are in danger, such as on a road or near water.
      3. Cushion the head with something soft, such as a folded jacket, and loosen anything tight around the neck.
      4. When the jerking stops, turn them on their side in the recovery position, to keep the airway clear.
      5. Stay with them until they have fully recovered. Speak gently and tell them what happened.

      What not to do:

      • Don’t put anything in their mouth, including a spoon, stick, wallet or your fingers. A person can’t swallow their tongue. In Malaysia, doctors still have to warn people against using a spoon. It can break teeth and block the airway.
      • Don’t hold them down or try to stop the jerking.
      • Don’t give food, water or pills until they are fully awake, as these can enter the lungs.
      • Don’t crowd them or try to wake them up.

      When to call 999

      • it is the person’s first seizure, or you don’t know whether it is
      • it lasts more than 5 minutes, or longer than is usual for them
      • another seizure starts without them recovering in between
      • they don’t recover fully, or they have trouble breathing
      • they are seriously injured
      • they have had three or more seizures in 24 hours

      A seizure lasting 5 minutes or more is called status epilepticus and is a medical emergency.

      What is a seizure?

      A seizure is a sudden burst of electrical activity in the brain that temporarily affects how it works. Not every seizure involves falling and jerking.

      TypeWhat it looks like
      Tonic-clonicThe person loses consciousness, goes stiff, falls and jerks, then is tired and confused afterwards
      FocalStarts in one part of the brain. There may be twitching, lip-smacking or strange smells or feelings, and the person may stay aware
      AbsenceA few seconds of blankness, more common in children, and easily mistaken for daydreaming

      Seizure or faint? Someone who has fainted usually wakes within about 30 seconds and recovers quickly. See our guide to low blood pressure and fainting. Call 999 if someone who has fainted is shaking or jerking, or hasn’t woken within a minute.

      What causes seizures?

      • Epilepsy
      • High fever in young children (see below)
      • Low blood sugar, especially in people taking insulin
      • Stroke, head injury, a brain tumour or infections such as meningitis and encephalitis
      • Alcohol or drug intoxication or withdrawal
      • Low sodium or high calcium levels in the blood
      • Eclampsia, a serious complication of pregnancy. A seizure in pregnancy or after birth is always an emergency.

      Seizures caused by a temporary problem are called “provoked” seizures and may not happen again once that problem is treated. In about half of people with epilepsy worldwide, no cause is found.

      Febrile seizures (sawan demam) in children

      Febrile seizures are fits triggered by a fever, usually in children aged 6 months to 6 years. They are frightening, but few children go on to develop epilepsy.

      Call 999 or go to an emergency department if it is the first one, it lasts more than 5 minutes, your child has trouble breathing, the twitching affects only one side, your child is still sleepy after an hour, or it happens again within 24 hours. Don’t try to cool your child by sponging. See our guide to fever in children.

      Epilepsy (epilepsi)

      Epilepsy generally means two or more unprovoked seizures, or one seizure with tests showing a high risk of more. A Malaysian community survey estimated that about 8 in 1,000 people have had epilepsy at some time, although that figure rests on a small number of confirmed cases.

      Tests: a detailed account from someone who saw the seizure is very useful, so try to film it if it is safe. Doctors may arrange an EEG (brainwave test), an MRI or CT scan, blood tests and a heart tracing (ECG). A normal EEG doesn’t rule out epilepsy.

      Treatment:

      • Anti-seizure medicines are the main treatment. Generic options include carbamazepine, lamotrigine, levetiracetam, oxcarbazepine and sodium valproate. The choice depends on the type of seizure, your age, and whether you might become pregnant.
      • Up to 70% of people can become seizure-free. More than 30% continue to have seizures despite treatment and may be referred to a specialist centre.
      • Surgery can help selected people whose seizures start in one area of the brain.
      • Ketogenic diets, under medical supervision, can help some people whose seizures don’t respond to medicines.
      • Stopping medicines: your doctor may discuss this after at least 2 years without a seizure, but seizures do come back in a substantial proportion of people. Reduce them only under specialist guidance.

      Rescue medicines: for long seizures, doctors may prescribe midazolam or rectal diazepam, and train family members in how to use them at home.

      SUDEP: sudden unexpected death in epilepsy happens in about 1 in 1,000 adults with epilepsy each year. The risk is highest in people with uncontrolled tonic-clonic seizures, particularly at night. Taking your medicines reliably is the most important way to reduce it.

      Words matter. The old Malay term gila babi is stigmatising and is no longer used by doctors. The recommended word is epilepsi.

      Living with epilepsy in Malaysia

      • Common triggers: missed medicines, lack of sleep, stress, alcohol and periods. Keep a seizure diary.
      • Safety at home: choose showers over baths, or have someone nearby when bathing. Swim only with someone who knows what to do. Avoid ladders and working at heights.
      • Pregnancy: Malaysia’s drug regulator warns that sodium valproate carries about a 10% risk of birth defects and up to a 30 to 40% risk of serious developmental problems. It should only be used in women if there is no alternative. Plan pregnancy with your doctor, ask about folic acid, and don’t stop your medicine without talking to your doctor first.
      • Driving: Malaysian law requires you to declare any condition likely to endanger the public when applying for or holding a licence, and knowingly driving with such a condition is an offence. Malaysian clinical guidelines suggest that driving may be allowed if epilepsy is well controlled and you have been seizure-free for at least a year, but public buses, heavy machinery and commercial flying are not allowed. Ask your neurologist and JPJ about your situation.
      • Support: Persatuan Epilepsi Malaysia and the Epilepsy Council of the Malaysian Society of Neurosciences provide information for patients and families.

      After a seizure

      A first seizure needs emergency assessment. Afterwards, a GP can review your medicines, check blood tests and arrange a referral to a neurologist. Our directory lists GP clinics across Malaysia, including those open late and 24 hours.

      Frequently asked questions

      What is sawan in English?

      Sawan means a seizure or fit. Sawan demam is a febrile seizure, a fit triggered by a fever in a young child.

      What should I do if someone has a seizure?

      Time it, move dangerous objects away, cushion the head, and stay with them. Turn them on their side once the jerking stops. Call 999 if it lasts more than 5 minutes or is a first seizure.

      Should I put a spoon in someone’s mouth during a seizure?

      No. Never put anything in their mouth. A person can’t swallow their tongue, and objects can break teeth or block the airway.

      Does one seizure mean I have epilepsy?

      Not necessarily. Many single seizures have a temporary cause. Epilepsy generally means two or more unprovoked seizures, or one with a high risk of more.

      How long can a seizure last before it is an emergency?

      Call 999 if it lasts more than 5 minutes, or longer than is usual for the person.

      Can epilepsy be cured?

      Up to 70% of people become seizure-free with treatment, and many children outgrow it. Some people can eventually stop medicines under specialist guidance.

      Can you drive with epilepsy in Malaysia?

      You must declare it. Malaysian clinical guidelines suggest driving may be allowed when epilepsy is well controlled and you have been seizure-free for at least a year. Ask your neurologist and JPJ.

      Is sawan dangerous?

      Most seizures stop by themselves within a few minutes. The dangers are injuries, seizures lasting more than 5 minutes, and rarely sudden death, so treatment and first aid both matter.
