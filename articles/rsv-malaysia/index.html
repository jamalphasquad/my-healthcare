      RSV in Malaysia: symptoms in babies and adults, danger signs, and the vaccine

      RSV is a common virus that almost every child catches by age 2. In most people it is just a cold, but in young babies it can inflame the small airways of the lungs (bronchiolitis) and cause serious breathing problems, and it can be dangerous for older adults too. Here is what to watch for, and how to protect the people most at risk.

      Key points
      • RSV spreads through coughs, sneezes, touch and contaminated surfaces. Symptoms start 4 to 7 days after contact, and most people recover in 1 to 2 weeks.
      • Babies under 6 months, premature babies, children with heart or lung conditions, older adults and people with weak immunity are most at risk of severe illness.
      • Get urgent help if a baby is breathing fast or with effort, has pauses in breathing, blue or grey lips, is feeding much less or has no wet nappy for 12 hours.
      • There is no specific medicine for RSV. Treatment is supportive: fluids, fever relief and, in hospital, oxygen.
      • In Malaysia, RSV vaccines for adults aged 60+ and pregnant women, and a preventive antibody injection for babies, are available privately. They aren’t part of the free national programme.

      What is RSV?

      Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) infects the nose, throat and lungs. It spreads through droplets from coughs and sneezes, by touching an infected person, and from contaminated hands, toys and surfaces, where it can survive for hours. Nearly all children have had RSV by age 2, and you can catch it again throughout life.

      RSV circulates all year in Malaysia, with peaks that often come in the second half of the year. A study of Negeri Sembilan’s government hospitals from 2017 to 2024 found that about 1 in 7 respiratory samples tested positive for RSV, with the usual peak between July and September.

      RSV symptoms in babies, children and adults

      Symptoms usually start 4 to 7 days after catching the virus:

      • Most children and adults: runny or blocked nose, cough, sneezing, sore throat, mild fever and tiredness, like a cold.
      • Babies: may be irritable, feed less than usual and have less energy. Not all have a fever. RSV can spread to the small airways (bronchiolitis), causing a worsening cough, wheezing and fast or difficult breathing, often worst around days 3 to 5.
      • Older adults: RSV can make heart and lung conditions such as asthma, COPD and heart failure worse, and can cause pneumonia or confusion.

      Most people recover within 1 to 2 weeks, although a cough can linger longer. See our guide to a cough that won’t go away.

      Get urgent medical help if a baby or child

      is breathing fast or working hard to breathe, with the skin pulling in under the ribs or at the neck; makes grunting noises or has pauses in breathing; has blue, grey or pale lips or tongue; is floppy, very sleepy or hard to wake; is feeding much less than usual or has had no wet nappy for 12 hours or more; is under 3 months old with a temperature of 38°C or above; or has a fit. Call 999 or go to an emergency department.

      Who is at risk of severe RSV?

      • babies under 6 months, especially in their first RSV season
      • babies born prematurely
      • children with heart or lung conditions, or a weakened immune system
      • adults aged 60 or 75 and over, especially with long-term heart or lung disease or diabetes
      • anyone with a weakened immune system

      Is RSV the same as flu or COVID-19?

      No. RSV, influenza and COVID-19 are different viruses, but their symptoms overlap, so only a test can tell them apart. Some clinics and laboratories offer swab tests that check for several viruses at once. Knowing which virus it is can matter for high-risk people, as flu and COVID-19 have antiviral treatments while RSV doesn’t. See our guide to influenza A.

      How RSV is treated

      There is no specific antiviral medicine for RSV, and antibiotics don’t work against it. Most people can be cared for at home:

      • Keep babies and children drinking: offer smaller, more frequent feeds.
      • Use saline drops and gentle suction to clear a blocked nose before feeds.
      • Give paracetamol (or ibuprofen for children old enough) for fever or discomfort, following the dose for their age. Don’t give aspirin to children under 16.
      • Keep children away from cigarette smoke.

      Babies and adults who struggle to breathe or drink may need hospital care for oxygen, fluids and help clearing secretions.

      RSV vaccines and baby injections in Malaysia

      Several RSV prevention options have become available in Malaysia since 2024. None is yet part of the free National Immunisation Programme, so they are given at private clinics and hospitals.

      WhoWhat is available
      Adults aged 60 and overRSV vaccines, given as a single dose. The first was approved in Malaysia in 2024.
      Pregnant womenAn RSV vaccine given in the third trimester, so the mother passes protective antibodies to her baby for the first months of life. Ask your obstetrician about the right timing.
      BabiesA preventive antibody injection (nirsevimab), given as a single dose before or during a baby’s first RSV season. It has been available in Malaysia since November 2025.

      Doctors’ groups in Malaysia have called for RSV protection for babies to be added to the national programme. Until then, talk to your doctor or paediatrician about whether it is suitable for your family, especially for premature or high-risk babies. Also see our baby vaccination schedule.

      Everyday prevention: wash hands often, keep sick people away from young babies, avoid kissing babies on the face when you have a cold, clean toys and surfaces, and keep children with cold symptoms home from nursery.

      Where to see a doctor about RSV

      For a baby or older adult with cold symptoms and any breathing difficulty, see a doctor promptly, or go to an emergency department if danger signs appear.       Frequently asked questions

      What are the symptoms of RSV in babies?

      A runny nose, cough, poor feeding, irritability and sometimes fever. In some babies it spreads to the lungs, causing wheezing and fast or difficult breathing. Get urgent help for breathing difficulty, pauses in breathing, blue lips or much reduced feeding.

      How long does RSV last?

      Most people recover in 1 to 2 weeks, though a cough can last longer. In babies, symptoms are often worst around days 3 to 5.

      Is RSV the same as flu?

      No. They are different viruses with similar symptoms, so only a test can tell them apart. Flu has antiviral treatments, while RSV doesn’t.

      How is RSV treated?

      There is no specific medicine. Treatment is supportive: fluids, fever relief, clearing a blocked nose and, for severe cases, oxygen and fluids in hospital.

      Is there an RSV vaccine in Malaysia?

      Yes. RSV vaccines are available privately for adults aged 60 and over and for pregnant women in the third trimester, and a preventive antibody injection is available for babies. They aren’t part of the free national programme.

      Is RSV contagious?

      Yes. It spreads through coughs, sneezes, touch and contaminated surfaces. People are usually infectious for 3 to 8 days, and young babies can spread it for longer.
