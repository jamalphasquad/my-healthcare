      Normal heart rate and palpitations (jantung berdebar): what’s normal, how to check your pulse, and when it’s a problem

      Your heart rate is the number of times your heart beats a minute. It changes all the time with activity, stress, caffeine and illness. Palpitations, a fluttering, pounding or skipping heartbeat (jantung berdebar), are very common and usually harmless. But some heart rhythm problems, especially atrial fibrillation, raise the risk of stroke and are worth finding.

      Key points
      • A normal resting heart rate for adults is 60 to 100 beats a minute. Very fit people may be in the 40s or 50s.
      • Check your pulse at rest: count the beats for 30 seconds and double it, or for a full minute if it feels irregular.
      • Palpitations are usually caused by caffeine, alcohol, nicotine, stress, poor sleep or exercise, and skipped beats are usually harmless.
      • An irregular pulse, especially from age 65, can be atrial fibrillation, which makes a stroke about 5 times more likely but can be treated.
      • Call 999 for palpitations with chest pain, breathlessness, fainting or dizziness, or a heartbeat that won’t settle.

      What is a normal heart rate? (Kadar degupan jantung normal)

      Adults: a normal resting heart rate is 60 to 100 beats per minute (bpm). Women tend to be slightly higher than men. Regular exercise lowers your resting rate, and athletes often have rates in the 40s or 50s.

      Children have faster heart rates. Typical ranges from the Royal Children’s Hospital Melbourne are:

      AgeHeart rate (bpm)
      Newborn120–170
      1 year105–150
      2 to 4 years80–150
      6 to 8 years70–140
      10 to 12 years60–130
      14 years and over60–115

      These ranges are wide, and a child’s heart rate rises with fever, crying and activity.

      What changes your heart rate: exercise, fever or infection, heat, dehydration, caffeine, alcohol, smoking, stress or anxiety, poor sleep, pregnancy, anaemia, an overactive thyroid and some medicines, including asthma inhalers (which raise it) and beta-blockers (which lower it).

      How to check your pulse

      1. Sit quietly for 5 minutes first. Avoid caffeine and smoking beforehand.
      2. Place two fingers (not your thumb) on the inside of your wrist, at the base of your thumb, or on your neck beside your windpipe.
      3. Count the beats for 30 seconds and double it. If the beat feels irregular, count for a full 60 seconds.
      4. Notice whether the rhythm is regular, like a ticking clock, or irregular.

      Smartwatches can measure your heart rate and some can alert you to an irregular rhythm. In a large study, only about a third of people who got an irregular-rhythm alert were found to have atrial fibrillation on a follow-up heart monitor. An alert is a reason to see a doctor, not a diagnosis, and no alert doesn’t rule out a problem.

      A home blood pressure monitor also shows your pulse. See our guide to checking your blood pressure at home.

      Fast and slow heart rates

      Fast (tachycardia): over 100 bpm at rest. Most often it is a normal response to exercise, fever, anxiety, caffeine, alcohol, dehydration, low blood sugar, anaemia or pregnancy. Sometimes it is a heart rhythm problem such as:

      • SVT (supraventricular tachycardia): sudden episodes of a very fast, regular heartbeat that start and stop abruptly, lasting minutes to hours. It is usually not serious. A doctor may teach you a breathing-and-straining technique (a modified Valsalva manoeuvre) to stop episodes, which worked in about 4 in 10 people in a UK trial.
      • Atrial fibrillation: see below.

      Slow (bradycardia): under 60 bpm. It is often normal in fit people and during sleep. A rate of 40 to 60 without symptoms is usually not a worry. It can also be caused by medicines such as beta-blockers, an underactive thyroid, sleep apnoea, ageing or heart conduction problems. A slow rate with dizziness, fainting, breathlessness or tiredness needs checking. A rate below 40 that isn’t normal for you, with symptoms, needs emergency care.

      Palpitations (jantung berdebar)

      Palpitations are when you become aware of your heartbeat: pounding, racing, fluttering or skipping. They are very common and usually harmless.

      Common triggers:

      • caffeine (coffee, tea, energy drinks), alcohol and nicotine, including vapes
      • stress, anxiety and panic. See our guide to anxiety.
      • exercise, and lack of sleep
      • pregnancy and the menopause
      • fever, dehydration and low blood sugar
      • medicines and products such as decongestants in cold remedies, asthma inhalers, thyroid medicines and some supplements
      • anaemia and an overactive thyroid

      Skipped or extra beats (ectopic beats) feel like a missed beat followed by a thump. According to the British Heart Foundation, they are usually harmless and don’t damage the heart. They are more noticeable with stress, poor sleep, alcohol and caffeine.

      Atrial fibrillation

      Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most common heart rhythm problem. The heart beats irregularly and often fast. Some people feel palpitations, breathlessness or tiredness, but many have no symptoms.

      • It becomes more common from age 55, and is more common in men, and in people with high blood pressure or heart disease.
      • AF makes a stroke about 5 times more likely, because blood clots can form in the heart. See our guide to stroke and heart attack.
      • A Malaysian study found AF in about 0.5% of adults aged 30 and over.
      • Treatment includes medicines to control the heart rate or rhythm, and blood-thinning medicines, which greatly lower stroke risk.
      • Asia Pacific heart rhythm experts recommend checking the pulse or an ECG opportunistically from age 65, for example at a routine clinic visit.

      Tests a doctor may do

      • an ECG (heart tracing)
      • a 24 to 48 hour heart monitor (Holter), or a longer patch monitor if symptoms come and go
      • an echocardiogram (heart ultrasound)
      • blood tests for anaemia, thyroid problems and salts such as potassium. See our guide to blood test results.

      Try to note when palpitations happen, how long they last, and whether they feel regular or irregular. If you can, record your pulse or an ECG on a smartwatch during an episode.

      When to get help

      Call 999 or go to an emergency department if palpitations:

      • come with chest pain, breathlessness, dizziness, or you faint or nearly faint
      • don’t stop
      • start during exercise

      See a doctor soon if:

      • palpitations keep coming back, or last more than a few minutes
      • your pulse is often irregular, especially if you are 65 or over
      • your resting heart rate is regularly above 100 or below 50 with symptoms
      • you have a heart condition, or a family history of heart problems or sudden death at a young age

      Where to get your heart checked

      A GP can check your pulse and blood pressure, do an ECG, arrange blood tests and a heart monitor, and refer you to a cardiologist if needed. Our directory lists GP clinics across Kuala Lumpur, Selangor and other states, including clinics open 24 hours.

      Frequently asked questions

      What is a normal heart rate?

      60 to 100 beats per minute at rest for adults. Very fit people may be lower, in the 40s or 50s.

      What is a normal heart rate for children?

      It is faster than in adults and falls with age, from about 120 to 170 in newborns to about 60 to 115 in teenagers.

      How do I check my pulse?

      Rest for 5 minutes, place two fingers on your wrist or neck, and count the beats for 30 seconds and double it, or count for 60 seconds if it feels irregular.

      Are palpitations dangerous?

      Usually not. Most are caused by caffeine, stress, alcohol or lack of sleep. Get urgent help if they come with chest pain, breathlessness or fainting.

      What causes skipped heartbeats?

      Extra (ectopic) beats, which are common and usually harmless, often triggered by stress, poor sleep, caffeine or alcohol.

      Is a heart rate of 110 at rest normal?

      It is higher than normal. It is often due to fever, dehydration, anxiety or caffeine, but if it stays high or comes with symptoms, see a doctor.

      What is atrial fibrillation?

      An irregular, often fast heart rhythm that raises stroke risk about 5 times. It is treatable with medicines, including blood thinners.

      Can a smartwatch detect heart problems?

      It can flag an irregular rhythm, but alerts aren’t a diagnosis. See a doctor for an ECG if you get one.
